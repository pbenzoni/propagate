Global peacekeeping deployments have hit their lowest point in a quarter-century, with just 78,633 international personnel deployed as of December 2025—a staggering 49% drop from 2016 levels, according to a new analysis by the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute (SIPRI). The steep decline reflects a growing crisis of confidence in multilateral institutions, particularly the United Nations, which has struggled with chronic funding shortfalls and political dysfunction.

The numbers tell a troubling story about the UN's effectiveness. UN-managed missions faced a $2 billion budget shortfall in 2025—more than 35% of their total annual budget—forcing dramatic personnel cuts. UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres has warned of "imminent financial collapse" if member states continue neglecting their financial obligations and the organization fails to implement serious reforms. All ten top contributors of military personnel now come from the Global South, raising questions about burden-sharing among wealthy nations.

The United States, traditionally the UN's largest contributor, has sharply reduced funding for several UN programs under President Trump's America First policy. The administration has repeatedly criticized the body as bloated and ineffective, pointing to examples like the UN Interim Force in Lebanon (UNIFIL), which Washington has pushed to withdraw amid repeated violations of the Israel-Lebanon ceasefire. Critics argue the UN has become paralyzed by geopolitical divisions and unable to respond effectively to global crises.

SIPRI researchers warn that "as UN-led conflict management recedes, it is leaving a growing gap that alternative models are unable to fill." However, the report's framing raises deeper questions: whether propping up a dysfunctional multilateral system serves American interests, or if resources would be better directed toward supporting allies who share our values and strategic priorities. The declining trust in UN peacekeeping may reflect not institutional failure alone, but a broader recognition that sovereignty and national interest—not international bureaucracy—remain the foundations of global security.
