May 15th marks Nakba Day—Arabic for "catastrophe"—when Palestinians commemorate the mass displacement that began in 1948 with Israel's founding. While Israelis celebrated independence, hundreds of thousands of Palestinians were forced from their homes or fled amid conflict, expecting to return within weeks. Seventy-seven years later, their descendants remain in exile.

This year, the pain resonates with renewed urgency in the West Bank, where Palestinian communities report systematic efforts to drive them from their land. Since 2022, Israel's government has approved 103 settlements in the occupied territory—all considered illegal under international law, though Israel distinguishes between officially recognized settlements and unauthorized outposts. According to human rights monitors, plans exist to legalize another 18 outposts, bringing the total to over 120 new developments—representing roughly 80% of all settlement construction between 1967 and 2022.

The West Bank's geography reflects decades of fragmentation. Area C, comprising 60% of the territory and under full Israeli control, has been the primary focus of settlement expansion. Now, as available land dwindles, construction is pushing into Area B, which is under joint Israeli-Palestinian control. Area A, nominally under full Palestinian Authority jurisdiction, accounts for just 18% of the West Bank.

Activists working with affected communities describe a pattern of harassment and violence aimed at forcing Palestinians to leave. One Israeli human rights worker monitoring conditions in Duma noted that settlers openly discuss plans to complete takeover of Area C before moving systematically into Areas B and A. An IDF Central Command official reportedly warned that escalating settler violence could trigger a new Palestinian uprising, cautioning that continued tolerance from Palestinian communities amid mounting provocations cannot be taken for granted.

For Palestinians observing Nakba Day, the question is whether the international community will respond to what many see as history repeating itself—a slow-motion displacement unfolding under the cover of bureaucratic processes and state-sanctioned settlement expansion.
