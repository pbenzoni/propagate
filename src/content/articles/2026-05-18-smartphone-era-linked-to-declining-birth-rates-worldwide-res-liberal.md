A comprehensive analysis of population data and technology adoption patterns has revealed a striking correlation between the widespread use of smartphones and declining birth rates across dozens of countries, according to reporting by the Financial Times.

Researchers examining the intersection of technology and demographics found that fertility rates dropped sharply in multiple nations following the mass adoption of smartphones, regardless of pre-existing demographic trends. A study by economists at the University of Cincinnati analyzing the rollout of 4G networks in the US and UK suggests that mobile devices fundamentally altered how young people spend time together, significantly reducing face-to-face social interaction during critical relationship-forming years.

The pattern appears globally consistent: birth rates among teenagers and young adults remained relatively stable in the early 2000s before declining after 2007 in developed nations, with similar drops occurring in France and Poland from 2009, followed by Mexico, Morocco and Indonesia around 2012, and later in Ghana, Nigeria and Senegal between 2013 and 2015.

Jesus Fernandez-Villaverde, an economics professor at the University of Pennsylvania studying demographic change, characterized falling fertility as "the big question of our time," noting that many contemporary economic and social challenges stem from collapsing birth rates. A recent Eurostat report projects the EU's population will decline by 11% over the next 75 years, falling below 400 million by century's end as fertility rates drop to approximately 1.3 children per woman.
