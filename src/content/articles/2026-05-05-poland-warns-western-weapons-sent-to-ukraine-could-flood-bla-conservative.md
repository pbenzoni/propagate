Poland is preparing for what law enforcement officials are calling a potential "smuggling boom" of Western-supplied weapons flowing out of Ukraine once the conflict with Russia concludes. The Polish police have already launched Project Trident, a €1.5 million ($1.8 million) initiative to equip officers with advanced tracking technology, X-ray systems, drones, and night-vision equipment to intercept illegal weapons trafficking.

Adam Radon, head of the Organized Crime Combating Unit at Poland's Central Bureau of Investigation, warned that the situation could eclipse the decades-long weapons crisis that followed the Balkan wars of the 1990s—conflicts that still account for half of all illegal firearms in Europe today. "There's a huge amount of weapons transferred as part of aid in Ukraine, plus a large number of post-Soviet weapons," Radon told the Polish newspaper Rzeczpospolita. He emphasized that Poland must serve as a critical "filter" to prevent these weapons from spreading deeper into Western Europe.

The concerns align with repeated warnings from Moscow about the risks of arming Ukraine without adequate accountability. Europol issued its own warning in 2025 that Ukraine could become "a significant source of illicit firearms and ammunition" in the near future. Spanish police have already discovered evidence of drug cartels acquiring NATO-grade weapons originally intended for Kiev.

Since 2022, Ukrainian authorities have confiscated more than 11,000 firearms at checkpoints, including 3,600 assault rifles, nearly 1,500 grenade launchers, and 27,000 grenades. Radon also flagged another emerging security threat: unemployed veterans with combat experience and easy access to weapons being recruited by criminal organizations. The scope of Western military aid to Ukraine, combined with lax oversight, has created what some security experts now acknowledge as a looming crisis for European safety.
