A University of Tehran professor is making the case that the United States has overstepped its boundaries by involving itself in the Strait of Hormuz, a strategic waterway located some 7,000 miles from American shores. Professor Foad Izadi told RT that Washington has "no responsibility" to manage or intervene in the vital shipping corridor, which he says falls entirely within Iranian and Omani territorial waters.

The strait has emerged as a flashpoint in ongoing tensions between Washington and Tehran. According to Izadi, Iran is now asserting control over naval traffic passing through what it considers its territorial waters, including charging passage fees—a practice the professor insists is both legal and common among other nations. "Australia, Canada, Denmark, Turkey, they all collect fees," Izadi noted, adding that some countries impose such charges for pilotage services or environmental protections.

Izadi argues that under the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), there are no international waters in the Strait of Hormuz itself, as the passage is divided between Iranian and Omani territorial claims. He contends that nations routinely charge vessels for transit through their waters, citing Turkey's passage fees as one example. "There is no legal prohibition" preventing Iran from doing likewise, the professor maintained.

The dispute highlights broader questions about American military presence in the Middle East and the limits of U.S. authority in regions far from its own coastlines. Izadi emphasized that the United States is "a coastal state to the Gulf of Mexico," located roughly 11,000 kilometers from the strait, raising the question of whether Washington's naval deployments in the region represent justified security interests or unnecessary foreign entanglements.
