Berlin is grappling with a severe infestation of oak processionary moth caterpillars—toxic pests that have forced park closures, health warnings, and mounting frustration among residents trapped by government inaction. The caterpillars, covered in hundreds of thousands of microscopic toxic hairs, can cause rashes, respiratory problems, and in severe cases, allergic shock.

The crisis has exposed deep failures in government coordination. Health agencies claim they lack authority to intervene because the caterpillars are classified as an "allergen" rather than a public-health pest, while environmental regulations restrict the use of effective pesticides. Multiple districts including Charlottenburg-Wilmersdorf, Treptow-Köpenick, and Spandau have seen sharp increases in affected trees this summer, yet bureaucratic gridlock has prevented meaningful action.

In one housing estate of over 11,000 residents, the infestation has spread to building facades, cars, door frames, and light fixtures. Frustrated locals have launched a petition demanding action, collecting more than 4,500 signatures. Critics are calling for earlier tree treatment and aggressive nest removal, but competing jurisdictions between district authorities, the Berlin Senate, and housing companies have paralyzed efforts.

Last year alone, Berlin recorded 5,032 infested oak trees at 881 locations citywide. Experts warn the outbreak will worsen as warm, dry weather favors the caterpillars' spread. The problem extends beyond Germany—the invasive species has established itself across much of Europe, including France, the Netherlands, and parts of the United Kingdom, where government warnings were issued Saturday urging the public to avoid contact with the insects.
