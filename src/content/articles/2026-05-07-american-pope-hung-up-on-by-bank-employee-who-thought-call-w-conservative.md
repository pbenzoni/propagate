Pope Leo XIV, the first American elected to lead the Catholic Church, was hung up on by a bank employee when he tried to update his personal account information after moving to the Vatican, according to reports confirmed by the pontiff's family.

The Chicago-born Leo, formerly known as Robert Francis Prevost, called his bank two months into his papacy to change his phone number and address. Despite correctly answering all security questions, the employee insisted he would need to visit a branch in person—an impossible demand for someone now residing at the Vatican.

When the Pope revealed his identity, saying "Would it matter to you if I told you I'm Pope Leo?" the bank employee apparently thought it was a joke and ended the call. The Pope's brother, John Prevost, confirmed the account, saying Leo had answered "four or five" security questions before being dismissed.

The incident highlights the absurd levels of bureaucratic red tape that even religious leaders face when dealing with modern financial institutions. The matter was eventually resolved after another priest contacted the bank president and threatened to move the Pope's account to a competitor—a reminder that sometimes the only language big institutions understand is the threat of lost business.
