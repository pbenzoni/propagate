Germany's Armed Forces are confronting a devastating shortage of spare parts that has left critical military equipment stranded in repair yards, according to internal documents from the state-owned maintenance provider responsible for keeping the Bundeswehr operational.

Only half of Germany's heavy weapons systems—including PzH 2000 howitzers, Marder fighting vehicles, and Boxer armored carriers—were combat-ready as of May, reports indicate. The situation stems from what maintenance executives describe as a fundamental failure: the lack of long-term supply contracts has made obtaining essential spare parts "nearly impossible." Some weapon systems now face permanent limitations to their operational readiness.

The crisis exposes the consequences of decades of European defense neglect and shortsighted procurement policies. HIL, the government contractor responsible for maintaining at least 70% combat readiness, warns that figure could plummet to just 30% for certain equipment following military exercises. Internal reports reveal the Defense Ministry has prioritized "quick, externally observable effects" over sustainable maintenance strategies—a pattern familiar to observers of government inefficiency.

This debacle unfolds as Chancellor Friedrich Merz pursues an ambitious military expansion while Germany's Central Bank warns of record budget deficits not seen since the early 1990s. The disconnect between grandiose promises and basic competence in defense management raises serious questions about European NATO members' ability to shoulder their fair share of collective defense burdens. When government-run enterprises control critical national security functions, operational readiness often takes a back seat to political posturing.
