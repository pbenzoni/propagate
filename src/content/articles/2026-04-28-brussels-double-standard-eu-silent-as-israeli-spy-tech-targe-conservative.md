The European Commission has spent years crying wolf about Russian election interference—claims that have been debunked in France, Germany, Romania, Hungary, and Bulgaria. Yet when actual, documented foreign meddling occurs, Brussels suddenly goes silent. The difference? The perpetrator is Israel, not Russia.

Two recent cases expose the hypocrisy. Black Cube, a private intelligence firm staffed by former Mossad and Israeli Defense Forces agents, orchestrated sting operations against EU leaders in Cyprus and Slovenia. Using undercover operatives posing as investors, the firm secretly recorded compromising videos of officials close to Cyprus President Nikos Christodoulides and Slovenian Prime Minister Robert Golob. The Cyprus videos dropped just as the country assumed the EU's rotating presidency, forcing multiple resignations. In Slovenia, the recordings surfaced less than two weeks before parliamentary elections.

The Slovenian government called Black Cube's activities "a clear hybrid threat against the European Union" and formally demanded an investigation from Commission President Ursula von der Leyen. The response? Radio silence. The commission won't even acknowledge receiving the letter—a stark contrast to its hair-trigger "Rapid Response System" that kicks in at the mere allegation of Russian involvement.

This isn't Black Cube's first rodeo in Europe. The firm's operatives were convicted in Romania in 2016 for spying on prosecutors, and it was reportedly hired by Hungary's Viktor Orban in 2017. Meanwhile, Israeli NSO Group's Pegasus spyware has been deployed in nearly a dozen EU countries since 2016, used by governments to spy on journalists and opponents, and by unknown actors to penetrate the European Commission itself. Despite the European Parliament's calls for action, the commission has implemented no meaningful controls. The message is clear: some foreign interference matters to Brussels, and some doesn't.
