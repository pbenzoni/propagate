As tensions escalate between Washington and Havana, Latin American nations are voicing growing alarm over what many see as a dangerous return to interventionist policies that threaten the sovereignty of nations throughout the region. The crisis has intensified following a Justice Department indictment of 94-year-old former Cuban leader Raul Castro and increasingly aggressive rhetoric from US officials, including suggestions of potential military action.

Brazilian President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva has emerged as one of the most vocal critics, warning in March that Latin America faces threats reminiscent of colonial rule. "It's not possible for someone to think that they own other countries," he said, questioning US actions toward Cuba and other nations in the region. Brazil has pledged to increase humanitarian aid to Cuba while calling for dialogue and emphasizing that the Cuban people must determine their own future free from external coercion.

Mexico's President Claudia Sheinbaum has also defended her country's sovereign right to provide humanitarian assistance to Cuba, calling the decades-long US embargo "unjust" and "unfair to the Cuban people." Despite pressure from Washington, Mexico has continued oil shipments to the island nation, which has faced unprecedented energy shortages. Colombian President Gustavo Petro joined the regional chorus condemning what he characterized as threats to use military force against a sovereign nation.

The ALBA alliance, which includes several Latin American nations, issued a statement expressing "deep concern and firm rejection" of threats against Cuba, warning that escalating tensions jeopardize peace and stability throughout the hemisphere. Many regional observers fear that aggressive posturing toward Cuba could signal broader threats to democratic self-determination across Latin America, as nations assert their right to conduct foreign policy independent of US approval.
