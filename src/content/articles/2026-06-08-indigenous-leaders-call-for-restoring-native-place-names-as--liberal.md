Indigenous leaders in Canada are advocating for the restoration of original place names across territories that were never legally ceded, particularly in British Columbia. Hereditary Chief Phil Lane Jr., a citizen of the Chickasaw and Ihanktonwan Nations, argues that returning to native nomenclature represents a meaningful step toward truth and reconciliation after centuries of systematic erasure.

Over 95% of British Columbia remains unceded territory—land never surrendered through treaty. When the province joined Canada in 1871, its government refused to recognize Aboriginal title or negotiate treaties across most of the region. Colonial authorities, witnessing Indigenous communities devastated by smallpox and other European diseases, assumed these populations would vanish entirely. Acting on this belief, they seized vast territories without consent, enforcing occupation through police and military power.

The human toll was staggering. Indigenous oral traditions indicate that over 1 million people lived in what is now British Columbia before European contact. Introduced diseases reduced that population to approximately 40,000. The Nuxalk Nation saw its numbers collapse from over 30,000 to around 300. The Tsleil-Waututh Nation near Vancouver declined from over 10,000 to fewer than 20 people.

The trauma extended beyond disease. Canada's residential school system, operated by churches with government funding, forcibly removed more than 150,000 Indigenous children from their families over a century. Children were punished for speaking their languages and forbidden from practicing their spiritual traditions, while facing widespread abuse. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission estimates that at least 25,000 children never returned home. The 2021 discovery of over 200 unmarked graves at the former Kamloops Indian Residential School brought renewed attention to this dark chapter.

Chief Lane emphasizes that restoring Indigenous place names is not an attack on Canada, but rather a modest acknowledgment of historical truth. Recent formal recognition of Haida Aboriginal Title across all of Haida Gwaii—10,180 square kilometers—confirms what Indigenous peoples have long maintained: their titles were never lawfully extinguished. For communities working to revitalize languages and heal from intergenerational trauma, reclaiming the names of ancestral places represents a fundamental act of dignity and justice.
