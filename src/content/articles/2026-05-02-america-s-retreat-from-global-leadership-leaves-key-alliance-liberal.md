With President Trump expected to meet Chinese leader Xi Jinping in Beijing this May, followed by a separate Xi-Putin summit, foreign policy analysts are questioning whether any meaningful international cooperation remains possible under the current administration's approach to global affairs.

For decades, the United States invested in building international institutions and partnerships that, while imperfect, created frameworks for addressing shared challenges. That strategic patience helped maintain relative stability and advanced democratic values worldwide. Now, experts observe a fundamental shift: the current administration prioritizes immediate, narrowly-defined national gains over the long-term investments in diplomacy and multilateral cooperation that previously anchored American foreign policy.

This transactional approach—openly dismissive of alliance-building and international norms—represents more than stylistic differences from previous administrations. It reflects a withdrawal from the leadership role America has played in promoting human rights, environmental protection, and democratic governance globally. While proponents argue this "America First" stance strengthens national interests, critics warn it cedes influence to authoritarian powers and undermines the very international structures that have served American security and prosperity.

The shift carries profound consequences beyond any single summit or negotiation. As one analysis from a recent international policy conference suggested, nations that once looked to Washington for partnership in addressing climate change, economic development, and security challenges are now recalibrating their expectations. The question facing allies and adversaries alike is whether the United States remains committed to collective solutions—or whether the world must adapt to a more fragmented, unstable international landscape where might increasingly makes right.
