Germany has dramatically reversed course on its once-welcoming asylum policy, now rejecting 95% of new Syrian asylum applications according to official government data. The shift marks a stark departure from the Merkel era's open-door approach, when approval rates for Syrian migrants routinely exceeded 90%.

The numbers come from a German government response to a parliamentary inquiry, revealing that in October 2025, the Federal Office for Migration and Refugees approved just 26 out of 3,134 Syrian asylum applications. The policy change follows the 2024 overthrow of Syrian leader Bashar Assad by former jihadist commander Ahmed al-Sharaa, with German authorities now arguing that blanket protection is no longer justified.

The reversal comes after mounting pressure from conservative parties, including the Alternative for Germany (AfD), which has consistently raised concerns about the strain on public services and public safety issues linked to mass migration. Those concerns were amplified by a series of violent incidents involving asylum seekers, including a 2024 knife attack in Solingen where a Syrian national killed three people and injured eight others.

German Chancellor Friedrich Merz initially stated that up to 80% of the nearly 1 million Syrians now living in Germany could return home over the next three years as part of reconstruction efforts, though he later walked back the specific figure. The stricter case-by-case assessments now being applied represent a significant policy shift as European nations grapple with the long-term consequences of earlier immigration decisions.
