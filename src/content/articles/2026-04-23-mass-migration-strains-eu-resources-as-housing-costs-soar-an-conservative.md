A new study reveals the European Union's immigrant population has reached an unprecedented 64.2 million in 2025, with 46.7 million born outside the EU entirely. This represents a staggering increase from roughly 40 million in 2010, according to data compiled by Berlin's Center for Research and Analysis on Migration using Eurostat and UN figures.

The burden falls unevenly across member states. Germany hosts nearly 18 million foreign-born residents, while Spain absorbed approximately 700,000 new arrivals last year alone—accounting for one-third of the EU's total increase. Smaller nations like Malta, Cyprus, and Luxembourg face the highest per-capita inflows, fundamentally transforming their communities.

Meanwhile, ordinary Europeans are paying the price. Official Eurostat data shows 8.2% of EU residents now spend at least 40% of their income just on housing, while nearly 17% live in overcrowded conditions and more than 9% cannot afford to adequately heat their homes. Among young people aged 15-29, nearly one in ten face crushing housing cost burdens as they compete for increasingly scarce resources.

European Council President Antonio Costa acknowledged that housing affordability sits "at the core of people's disillusionment with democratic institutions." Yet Brussels dedicates only 2% of its seven-year budget to migration and border management, leaving national governments—and ultimately taxpayers—to shoulder most costs. Growing anti-immigration sentiment reflects citizens' legitimate concerns over housing shortages, strained public services, and rising cost of living that elites have failed to address.
