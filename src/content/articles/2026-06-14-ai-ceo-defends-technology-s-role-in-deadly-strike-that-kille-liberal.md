The CEO of Anthropic, a leading artificial intelligence company, has stated that his firm's technology being used in a U.S. military strike that killed nearly 160 people—predominantly children—did not violate the company's ethical guidelines.

The strike targeted a girls' elementary school in Minab, Iran, on the first day of a February conflict. According to reports, the attack relied on Palantir's surveillance software, which incorporates Anthropic's Claude AI, and was based on what sources describe as outdated intelligence data.

In a Bloomberg interview published Wednesday, CEO Dario Amodei addressed concerns about his company's role in the deadly incident. "We don't know exactly how these models were used… and what you're talking about is a use case that doesn't even violate our red lines," he said. Amodei emphasized that while AI assists military operations, "a human made that final call," and maintained his company opposes fully autonomous weapons systems.

The incident has reignited urgent questions about tech industry accountability and the ethics of AI deployment in warfare. Critics point to what they describe as a troubling pattern: major U.S. tech companies—including Google, Amazon, Microsoft, and OpenAI—have recently signed Pentagon contracts for AI services. A former Google whistleblower characterized the industry's position as a dangerous "catch-22," warning that companies are essentially arguing that without unrestricted access to data and deregulation, civilian casualties become inevitable.

Advocates for AI regulation argue the tragedy underscores the need for stronger oversight of how artificial intelligence is deployed in life-and-death situations, particularly when corporate "red lines" appear insufficient to prevent catastrophic outcomes.
