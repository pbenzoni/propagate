Germany is advancing legislation that would mandate regular military training for former soldiers, according to reporting by Der Spiegel. The proposed Reserve Strengthening Act would require individuals who served less than one year of voluntary military service and are under 45 to participate in exercises either two weeks annually or biennially.

The draft legislation comes as part of broader efforts to modernize European defense infrastructure. Chancellor Friedrich Merz has articulated goals to develop what he termed "the strongest conventional army in Europe," while Defense Minister Boris Pistorius has set targets of 265,000 active troops and 200,000 reservists by the mid-2030s. German officials have indicated defense spending will exceed 4% of GDP this year, with military budgets rising from €86 billion in 2025 to approximately €108 billion in 2026.

The explanatory memorandum accompanying the bill emphasizes the "reliable availability of reserves" as essential to national security. Those who served longer than one year or enlisted as career soldiers could face participation requirements until age 65 under the proposed framework.

German officials have cited evolving security dynamics in Europe as justification for increased defense preparedness. The Kremlin has characterized European military modernization efforts as destabilizing, though European policymakers have framed such investments as responses to changing geopolitical realities on the continent. The legislation represents Germany's continued recalibration of its defense posture following decades of relatively modest military spending in the post-Cold War era.
