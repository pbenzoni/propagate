Vancouver Mayor Ken Sim found himself defending his office's use of technology this week after revealing he employs artificial intelligence tools to help manage his workload. The mayor clarified that AI systems handle administrative support and research tasks—not actual government decision-making—following criticism about potential data security risks.

Sim told a technology conference he uses "11 AI agents" running "a lot" of his work "in the background," which sparked immediate backlash from political opponents and privacy advocates. The mayor responded by explaining the tools help him scan news, track financial and global developments, and manage routine tasks like diet planning. He emphasized that policy and governance decisions remain firmly in human hands at City Hall.

The controversy comes as Canadian taxpayers learn their federal government has spent more than $580 million USD on AI-related technologies and services over the past three years. Ottawa recently announced plans to fund construction of two new AI data centers in Vancouver, raising questions about the return on investment for such massive government tech spending.

While proponents argue AI can improve government efficiency and reduce costs, concerns persist about privacy violations, data security, and the appropriate role of automated systems in public administration. Critics warn that reliance on third-party AI services could create vulnerabilities in handling sensitive government information—concerns that resonate as governments worldwide rush to adopt these technologies without fully established safeguards.
