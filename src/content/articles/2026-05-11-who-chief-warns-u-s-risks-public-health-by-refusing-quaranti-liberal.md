The World Health Organization's chief has raised concerns about the United States' approach to handling passengers from a hantavirus-stricken cruise ship, warning that the decision "may have risks" to public health.

While the WHO recommends a 42-day quarantine period for all 147 passengers and crew aboard the MV Hondius—where three people have died from the rare pathogen—U.S. health officials announced they would only conduct brief health checks at Nebraska's National Quarantine Unit. Passengers showing no symptoms will be allowed to return home immediately, rather than isolating for the recommended period.

The approach stands in stark contrast to protocols adopted by America's European allies. Both the United Kingdom and France have mandated quarantine periods of 42 and 45 days respectively for their nationals, either at staffed facilities or in home isolation. WHO Director-General Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus emphasized during a Sunday press conference in Tenerife that while the organization doesn't impose requirements on member states, it does provide guidance on "what it considers the most appropriate way to deal with the situation."

The divergent response highlights ongoing tensions between U.S. health policy and international public health recommendations. The United States officially withdrew from the WHO in January, citing accusations of pandemic mismanagement—a move that critics argued would undermine global cooperation on disease prevention and response.

Only one American passenger, who tested positive for hantavirus, will be placed in a biocontainment unit. The virus, typically spread through contact with infected rodent droppings, urine, or saliva, has limited human-to-human transmission according to Ghebreyesus, though he acknowledged the pathogen remains "serious."
