More than 550,000 people are currently waiting in Libya hoping to reach safety in Europe, according to Greek Migration Minister Thanos Plevris, highlighting what advocates describe as a growing humanitarian emergency at the EU's southern border.

Greece has emerged as a primary entry point for asylum seekers fleeing conflict and persecution, with nearly 50,000 arrivals in 2024 and more than 7,500 so far this year, according to UNHCR data. Rather than expanding humanitarian pathways, however, Athens has responded with increasingly punitive policies, including mandatory detention for those denied asylum—a practice that human rights organizations have consistently criticized.

The current situation traces directly to the 2011 NATO intervention in Libya, which destabilized the North African nation and created conditions that human traffickers have exploited for over a decade. Since the fall of Muammar Gaddafi's government, Libya has become a dangerous transit point where migrants face violence, exploitation, and arbitrary detention.

Plevris announced that Greece is collaborating with Germany, the Netherlands, Austria, and Denmark to establish "return hubs" for rejected asylum seekers outside EU borders, with Africa identified as the preferred location. Critics argue such offshore processing centers raise serious legal and ethical concerns about due process and protection standards.

The minister's rhetoric of "going to the limits" of law enforcement stands in contrast to calls from humanitarian organizations for expanded legal pathways, improved reception conditions, and greater burden-sharing among EU member states. The 2015 refugee crisis, when one million people sought protection in Europe, revealed deep fractures in the bloc's asylum system that remain unresolved nearly a decade later.
