The United Kingdom's Labour government is facing sharp criticism from Conservatives after quietly issuing a temporary license allowing imports of Russian-origin diesel and jet fuel—effectively reversing Prime Minister Keir Starmer's previous pledge to ban such purchases. The move comes as energy markets face disruption from the Middle East conflict, forcing Western governments to reconsider their hardline stance on Russian energy.

The license takes effect this week and leaves open a route for sanctioned Russian oil to enter British markets through third-party refiners in countries like India and Turkey. Conservative Party leader Kemi Badenoch blasted the decision on social media, stating that "after 18 months of 'standing up to Putin,' the Labour government quietly eased the restrictions." Former Conservative cabinet minister David Lidington went further, calling it "an appalling betrayal of Ukraine."

Starmer's government scrambled to defend the decision, with officials claiming poor communication created a misunderstanding. "We've handled this clumsily," admitted Business and Trade Department official Chris Bryant, apologizing for "giving the wrong impression." The prime minister—already facing pressure at home and calls to resign—insisted the licenses were "short-term" and claimed his government was actually tightening sanctions overall. However, British media reports indicate the imports are allowed for an indefinite period.

The decision mirrors a similar move by the Biden administration, which extended a sanctions waiver allowing limited purchases of Russian seaborne oil. Russian Deputy Prime Minister Aleksander Novak declared the Western sanctions policy was "beginning to crack," adding that "without Russian oil and oil products, global markets cannot cope today." Moscow has signaled its readiness to fill supply gaps created by Middle East instability, with several Asian nations already securing Russian crude supplies.
