The Scottish Parliament has voted to formally request permission from London to hold another independence referendum, despite clear signals that the Labour government will reject the demand. Pro-independence parties—the Scottish National Party (SNP) and Scottish Greens—hold 73 of 129 seats at Holyrood and backed First Minister John Swinney's call for a so-called "indyref2."

Scottish voters decisively rejected independence in 2014, a referendum that unionist politicians described as a "once in a generation" opportunity. Yet separatist movements have continued pushing for another vote, particularly after Brexit, which most Scottish voters opposed in 2016. The Scottish government is requesting a Section 30 order under the Scotland Act 1998, the same mechanism used to authorize the 2014 referendum.

Prime Minister Keir Starmer's government has made clear it will not grant permission for another vote. Downing Street urged Scottish leaders to focus "on the issues that really matter—economic growth, the cost of living, and public services" rather than pursuing what critics call an "obsession" with independence. Unlike 2014, there is currently no public consensus in favor of holding another referendum.

Swinney has challenged Westminster to prove "this is a voluntary Union" by allowing Scots to vote again on whether to remain in the post-Brexit UK. However, previous prime ministers from both parties—including Boris Johnson and Theresa May—have rejected similar requests. Scottish Conservative leader Russell Findlay and others have criticized the vote as a distraction from pressing economic and social concerns facing ordinary Scots.
