Donald Trump's recent visit to Beijing—his first trip to the Chinese capital in nearly a decade—was marked by elaborate ceremonies and warm rhetoric, but appeared to produce minimal substantive agreement on key issues dividing Washington and Beijing.

Chinese President Xi Jinping rolled out the red carpet for Trump with military honors, a banquet, and a personal tour of his Zhongnanhai compound. Xi spoke optimistically about U.S.-China relations, while Trump praised the "honor" of his welcome and called the roses in Xi's gardens "the most beautiful roses anyone has ever seen." Despite the pageantry, the two leaders' differing post-summit statements suggest deep disagreements remain on Taiwan, Iran policy, and trade.

On Taiwan—the most sensitive issue in U.S.-China relations—Xi reportedly warned that mishandling the question could lead the two nations toward "collision or even conflict." Trump told reporters he "made no commitment either way" regarding Taiwan's defense, maintaining the longstanding U.S. policy of strategic ambiguity. "We're supposed to travel 9,500 miles to fight a war?" Trump asked in a Fox News interview, expressing reluctance about military involvement.

Regarding Iran, Trump claimed he and Xi reached similar conclusions, with the White House asserting that Xi opposed militarization of the Strait of Hormuz and expressed interest in purchasing American oil. However, China's Foreign Ministry statement made no mention of nuclear weapons, tolls, or oil purchases—revealing a significant gap between American and Chinese characterizations of the discussion.

The summit underscored the complex and often fraught nature of U.S.-China relations, where diplomatic niceties can mask profound policy differences. While both leaders praised their personal relationship, the lack of concrete agreements suggests that resolving fundamental tensions between the world's two largest economies remains elusive.
