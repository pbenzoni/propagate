The economic shockwaves from Middle Eastern conflict are now washing ashore in unexpected places. Indonesian fishing crews report that soaring diesel prices—linked to disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz amid escalating tensions involving Iran—have nearly doubled their fuel costs, forcing fishing fleets to remain docked for months while hardworking families lose their primary income.

Fishermen in Jakarta explained that larger vessels simply cannot afford to operate anymore. Boats that once spent weeks at sea now sit idle in port. "Mostly it is disrupted, sir, from the income, which usually comes every three months or four months," one fisherman said. Crews now need to catch approximately $40,000 worth of squid just to break even on operating costs—a threshold many cannot reach. Even subsidized diesel for smaller boats has become scarce.

The crisis has sparked protests, with hundreds of fishermen in Central Java demanding relief earlier this month. Local officials warn that rising fuel costs threaten to disrupt fish supplies and drive up inflation, hurting everyday consumers. The situation illustrates how instability in strategic chokepoints like the Strait of Hormuz—through which roughly one-fifth of global oil passes—can devastate working-class communities across the globe.

Facing the fuel emergency, Indonesian President Prabowo Subianto traveled to Moscow last month to secure a deal for up to 150 million barrels of discounted Russian oil. The country's Energy Minister indicated that the first shipments could arrive within one to two weeks, offering some hope to struggling fishing communities that depend on affordable energy to earn an honest living.
