A newly unveiled European Union age-verification application is raising serious privacy concerns among digital rights advocates and security researchers, who warn the technology could pave the way for expanded government surveillance of internet users.

The app, presented by European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen as meeting "the highest privacy standards," was reportedly compromised by security researchers in under two minutes. Security consultant Paul Moore told Cybernews the app contains fundamental design flaws that make it vulnerable to exploitation, warning it could become "the catalyst for an enormous breach."

Civil liberties advocates have long cautioned that mandatory age-verification systems create centralized databases of personal information—so-called "honeypots"—that become attractive targets for both hackers and government overreach. Similar concerns have emerged in the UK, where a proposed digital ID scheme has drawn criticism from privacy advocates warning about the erosion of anonymity online.

The EU initiative is part of a broader international trend, with Australia recently banning social media for users under 16, and several European nations including Denmark, France, and Spain piloting their own age-verification technologies. Critics argue these systems, while ostensibly protecting minors, threaten the privacy rights of all internet users by normalizing mandatory identity verification for online activity.

Digital rights organizations have consistently warned that such tools can be repurposed for broader surveillance once the infrastructure is in place, transforming privacy-protective measures into mechanisms for tracking citizens' online behavior.
