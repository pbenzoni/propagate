Iran announced plans to pursue legal accountability against the United States and Israel for attacks that damaged more than 149 historical landmarks and museums across 20 provinces, according to Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi. Among the damaged sites are five locations registered with UNESCO as world heritage properties.

Gharibabadi emphasized that Iran's cultural heritage "is not merely a national asset of the Iranian people; it is part of humanity's shared memory." The Persian civilization, one of the world's oldest continuous cultures dating to the Achaemenid Empire founded in 550 BC, has left an irreplaceable archaeological and historical record. The deputy minister stated that Tehran would "register, document, and pursue this assault on its cultural heritage within the framework of international responsibility."

The 1954 Hague Convention and fundamental principles of humanitarian law explicitly protect cultural sites during armed conflicts. Iran's Ministry of Culture has estimated repair costs at approximately $39 million. However, holding the U.S. accountable in international courts faces significant obstacles due to Washington's refusal to recognize compulsory jurisdiction of global legal bodies and the doctrine of sovereign immunity.

The announcement comes amid a fragile ceasefire established in early April following intense hostilities, though indirect peace negotiations have stalled. In recent statements that drew international criticism, including from UN Secretary General António Guterres, President Trump warned that Iran's "whole civilization will die" if it fails to accept American demands—rhetoric Pope Leo XIV called "truly unacceptable." The Iranian Defense Ministry has stated it remains prepared to defend against potential future attacks.
