Iran's deputy foreign minister announced plans to pursue legal action against the United States and Israel over alleged damage to cultural heritage sites during recent military operations. Kazem Gharibabadi claimed that at least 149 historical landmarks and museums across 20 Iranian provinces, including five UNESCO-registered sites, were damaged in strikes.

The threat of litigation comes as a fragile ceasefire holds between the parties, though peace negotiations remain stalled with both sides rejecting each other's terms. Iranian officials estimate repair costs at approximately $39 million and argue the damage violates the 1954 Hague Convention protecting cultural sites during armed conflict.

Legal experts note that actually holding the United States accountable in international courts faces significant practical obstacles. The doctrine of sovereign immunity and America's non-recognition of compulsory jurisdiction by global legal bodies makes successful litigation unlikely. The U.S. has historically not been held liable for heritage site damage in previous military operations spanning from Korea to Afghanistan.

President Trump has maintained firm pressure on Tehran, warning of severe consequences if Iran fails to accept American demands. The Iranian Defense Ministry responded by stating it remains "fully prepared" to counter any renewed attacks. International figures including UN Secretary General Antonio Guterres have expressed concern over escalating rhetoric in the region.
