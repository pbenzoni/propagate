Google has finalized an agreement to provide artificial intelligence tools to the Pentagon for use on classified military networks, expanding a $200 million contract signed earlier this year. The deal allows the Department of Defense to deploy Google's Gemini AI for "any lawful governmental purpose," including mission planning and weapons targeting—raising alarm bells among advocates for responsible technology development.

More than 600 Google employees, including senior staff members, have signed an open letter urging CEO Sundar Pichai to halt military applications of the company's AI systems. The letter warns that without stronger safeguards, the technology could be used in "inhumane or extremely harmful ways." Workers are calling for a moratorium on such contracts, greater transparency around military partnerships, and the establishment of an independent ethics board to oversee these collaborations.

The agreement notably permits the Pentagon to request modifications to Google's safety filters, while clarifying that the company cannot veto decisions deemed "lawful" by the government. Critics argue this structure sidesteps meaningful oversight and accountability—concerns that resonate with a broader movement among tech workers pushing back against the militarization of AI. Google spokesperson Jenn Crider stated the company "remains committed to ensuring AI is not used for domestic mass surveillance or autonomous weapons without human oversight," though the contract's broad language has done little to assuage internal dissent.

The Pentagon has been aggressively securing access to cutting-edge AI systems from multiple developers, citing the need for flexibility and reduced dependence on any single vendor. Other firms, including OpenAI, have signed similar deals. However, AI startup Anthropic recently refused to loosen safeguards related to surveillance and autonomous weapons—a decision that led the Defense Department to label it a "supply-chain risk" and effectively exclude it from future contracts. Anthropic has since challenged that determination in court.

Activists and tech workers have staged protests outside company offices, holding signs reading "No AI surveillance state" and invoking warnings about unchecked government power. While Pentagon officials insist they have no plans to use AI for mass domestic surveillance or fully autonomous weaponry, the scope of these contracts and the lack of independent oversight continue to fuel debate over where the line should be drawn between national security and civil liberties.
