The Caribbean is heating up again. As Washington maintains maximum pressure on the communist Castro regime through sanctions and increased military presence, defense analysts are examining potential scenarios should the decades-long standoff escalate beyond diplomacy. The trigger isn't just fresh accusations against Raúl Castro or the highly publicized USS Nimitz carrier group deployment—it's the broader trajectory of a regime that continues to threaten American interests and regional stability.

While the administration officially denies planning a full-scale military operation, the worsening crisis makes contingency planning essential. An executive order signed January 29, 2026, formally classified Havana's policies as a national security threat. Cuba remains designated as a state sponsor of terrorism, cutting it off from international finance and foreign investment. The question defense experts are asking: What would America's options look like if diplomatic and economic pressure fails?

From a military standpoint, the groundwork already exists. General Francis Donovan, commander of U.S. Southern Command, testified to Congress in March 2026 that combined regional forces maintain robust operations using naval assets, aerial surveillance, and special operations units. The U.S. naval base at Guantánamo Bay provides a critical platform for projecting American power. More than 10,000 U.S. personnel currently operate in the Caribbean theater.

Cuba maintains armed forces of roughly 50,000 active personnel, equipped with aging Soviet-era equipment including 200 tanks and 500 artillery systems. More significantly, the regime continues preparing for asymmetric warfare under its "War of the Entire People" doctrine—a strategy of total civilian mobilization designed to drag any conflict into a prolonged guerrilla campaign. This presents unique challenges for U.S. planners.

Analysts outline several potential scenarios, from limited precision strikes targeting regime leadership and command infrastructure using Tomahawk cruise missiles and special operations forces, to broader campaigns aimed at degrading Cuban military capabilities. The greatest concern in any scenario is escalation risk—particularly the possibility of Cuban retaliation against Guantánamo Bay and the international fallout from direct military action. Even limited strikes could spiral into larger commitments, forcing difficult decisions about American involvement in yet another protracted conflict.
