German Chancellor Friedrich Merz, long considered one of Europe's most pro-American conservative leaders, delivered a sobering assessment of the United States at a Catholic youth conference last week: he would no longer recommend America as a destination for his own children to study or work.

Speaking in Wuerzburg, Merz pointed to what he called a deteriorating "social climate" marked by deep polarization and diminishing economic opportunity. "I am a great admirer of America," he acknowledged during the panel discussion. "At the moment my admiration is not growing."

The chancellor's concerns about employment prospects appear grounded in troubling data. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York recently reported that unemployment among young college graduates aged 22–27 has reached 5.7%, while underemployment—working in jobs that don't require a degree—stands above 41%. Nearly half of recent graduates now find themselves in positions unrelated to their education, as white-collar hiring slows amid corporate cost-cutting and AI-driven workforce changes.

Merz's critique carries particular weight given his background working with American financial institutions like BlackRock and his longtime advocacy for strong transatlantic ties. His remarks come during a period of strained U.S.-German relations, exacerbated by recent tensions over foreign policy and the Pentagon's announcement that it would withdraw 5,000 troops from Germany—though around 31,000 American personnel will remain in the country, underscoring the complex interdependence that persists despite growing diplomatic friction.
