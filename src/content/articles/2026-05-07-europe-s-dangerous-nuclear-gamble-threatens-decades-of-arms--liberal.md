A troubling transformation is unfolding across European capitals as leaders increasingly turn to nuclear weapons as tools of statecraft, raising alarm among arms control advocates and those committed to diplomatic conflict resolution. What officials frame as necessary deterrence represents a fundamental departure from decades of restraint that helped maintain continental stability.

French President Emmanuel Macron has spearheaded this shift, promoting what he calls "advanced deterrence"—a doctrine that would extend France's nuclear umbrella to European partners. France is expanding its arsenal while simultaneously reducing long-standing transparency commitments, moves that undermine the norms of accountability essential to preventing catastrophic miscalculation. These developments integrate nuclear weapons into EU policy in unprecedented ways, normalizing their role rather than treating them as last-resort instruments requiring extreme caution.

Germany's evolution proves equally concerning for those who value the country's post-war commitment to peaceful conflict resolution. Berlin is now participating in French nuclear exercises and establishing joint strategic coordination mechanisms—steps that, while technically legal, represent a profound political shift away from the restraint that defined German foreign policy for generations. This transformation appears driven more by reactive fear than careful strategic thinking.

Poland has added urgency to these discussions, with Prime Minister Donald Tusk speaking of nuclear autonomy despite the country's non-nuclear status and treaty obligations. While some Polish officials acknowledge that European arrangements cannot replace existing security frameworks and warn against overconfidence, the broader discourse reflects how quickly escalation has become normalized in policy circles. Critics argue this embrace of nuclear brinkmanship abandons diplomacy precisely when de-escalation and dialogue remain essential to European security.
