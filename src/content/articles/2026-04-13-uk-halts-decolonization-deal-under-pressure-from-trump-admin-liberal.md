The United Kingdom has paused a planned transfer of the Chagos Islands to Mauritius following opposition from the Trump administration, raising questions about America's commitment to international decolonization efforts and self-determination.

British Prime Minister Keir Starmer had signed an agreement in 2024 to transfer sovereignty of the Indian Ocean archipelago to Mauritius while preserving joint U.S.-UK military operations on Diego Garcia Island through a 99-year lease arrangement. However, President Trump characterized the sovereignty transfer as a "big mistake" in February, despite the agreement's protection of American military interests in the region.

A UK government spokesperson stated Sunday that Britain would only proceed "if it has US support," effectively giving Washington veto power over the decolonization agreement. The move comes despite a UN resolution supporting Mauritius's sovereignty claim and decades of international pressure to address the forced displacement of the Chagossian people, who were expelled from their homeland in the 1960s to make way for the military base.

The Chagos issue has long symbolized the colonial era's unfinished business. In 1965, Britain separated the archipelago from Mauritius prior to the latter's independence, compensating the new nation with just £3 million. The UK then leased Diego Garcia to the United States in 1966 in exchange for a discount on weapons purchases, establishing a military installation that has supported operations across the Middle East and Asia.

Mauritius has scheduled talks with UK officials for April 22 to address the suspended agreement. Meanwhile, conservative opposition parties in Britain have opposed the transfer, despite the deal's preservation of military access—an arrangement that appeared to balance sovereignty concerns with strategic interests until the Trump administration's intervention.
