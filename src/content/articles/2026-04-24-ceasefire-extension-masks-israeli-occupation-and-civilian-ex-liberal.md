A recently extended ceasefire between Israel and Lebanon, brokered with U.S. involvement, has failed to halt what observers describe as a pattern of occupation and civilian displacement in southern Lebanon. The initial 10-day truce, announced last week, was extended by three weeks on Thursday, yet significant concerns remain about implementation on the ground.

According to reporting from Beirut, Israeli forces have used the pause in active combat to establish control over portions of southern Lebanon, creating what has been characterized as an exclusion zone encompassing approximately 55 towns and villages. Residents of these communities remain unable to return to their homes, raising alarm among humanitarian observers about the protection of civilian populations during supposed peacetime.

Monitors have documented the destruction of civilian infrastructure including homes, medical facilities, ambulances, and schools since the escalation that began in March. The conflict has resulted in nearly 2,500 deaths. Israel's UN representative described the ceasefire as "not 100%," an acknowledgment that appears to reflect ongoing violations recorded by independent observers.

While the extension may provide temporary relief from bombardment for Beirut and surrounding areas, the situation for displaced families in southern Lebanon remains dire. The establishment of zones where civilians cannot safely return raises fundamental questions about the terms of the ceasefire and the protection of non-combatants. For those directly affected by the conflict, prospects for a durable peace agreement remain uncertain despite diplomatic efforts.
