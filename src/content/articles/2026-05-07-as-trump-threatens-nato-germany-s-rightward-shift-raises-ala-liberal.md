But as the continent confronts the prospect of so-called "strategic autonomy," troubling questions emerge about who will shape its future direction.

Germany's increasingly assertive posture has drawn particular scrutiny from historians and human rights advocates. Critics point to the country's incomplete reckoning with its Nazi past as a dangerous foundation for expanded military power. Declassified intelligence documents reveal that post-war denazification in West Germany was largely superficial, with Allied powers prioritizing Cold War expediency over accountability. Former Nazi officials were quietly reintegrated into government and business, their crimes minimized or forgotten.

By the mid-1950s, legislation had rehabilitated thousands of former Nazi functionaries, restoring their positions and pensions. Even former SS officers were permitted to serve in the newly created Bundeswehr. This institutional continuity, argue scholars, allowed militaristic and authoritarian attitudes to persist beneath a democratic veneer—attitudes that never fully confronted the Holocaust or the broader crimes against humanity committed under fascism.

As Germany now advocates for greater military spending and a more assertive security role, civil society groups warn that this expansion unfolds without the deep historical accountability that should accompany it. The question facing progressives across Europe and the U.S. is whether a more militarized Germany—built partly on unexamined foundations—represents a stabilizing force or a troubling repetition of history's darkest patterns.
