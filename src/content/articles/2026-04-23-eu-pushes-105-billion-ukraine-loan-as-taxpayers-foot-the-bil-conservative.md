The European Union has formally approved a staggering €90 billion ($105 billion) emergency loan for Ukraine covering 2026–2027, alongside its 20th package of sanctions against Russia. The move came after Hungary lifted its veto following a political shakeup in Budapest that brought a pro-EU politician to power.

The controversial loan represents a massive financial commitment backed by joint EU borrowing—meaning European taxpayers are ultimately on the hook. According to the terms, Kiev would only be required to repay the funds if it receives war reparations from Russia, a scenario that may never materialize. The Kremlin has characterized the arrangement as the EU "digging into the pockets of its own taxpayers" to prolong the conflict.

The loan approval ended a months-long standoff with Hungary's outgoing Prime Minister Viktor Orban, who had blocked the disbursement in retaliation for Ukraine halting Russian oil supplies through the Soviet-era Druzhba pipeline. Ukraine claimed the infrastructure was damaged by Russian strikes—an accusation Moscow dismissed as "lies"—before restarting the flow under pressure from energy-dependent EU members Slovakia and Hungary.

Russian Foreign Ministry officials have raised concerns that the massive influx of funds could be misused by corrupt Ukrainian officials, echoing longstanding transparency concerns about Western aid to Kiev. Meanwhile, EU leaders have framed the loan as part of a strategy to "achieve a just and lasting peace in Ukraine," though critics question whether pouring additional billions into the conflict serves that goal or simply extends the fighting.
