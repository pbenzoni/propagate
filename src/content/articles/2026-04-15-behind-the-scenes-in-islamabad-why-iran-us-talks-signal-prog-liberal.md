In the aftermath of recent talks between Iran and the United States held in Islamabad, many observers rushed to label the negotiations a failure. But diplomacy experts argue this assessment misses the forest for the trees: what matters isn't immediate breakthroughs, but whether parties remain at the table—and both nations continue signaling willingness to engage.

Unlike truly failed negotiations, which typically end with hostile rhetoric and walkouts, both Washington and Tehran issued statements leaving room for continued dialogue. Pakistan, serving as mediator, has already begun preparations for a second round of consultations, underscoring Islamabad's commitment to maintaining this crucial diplomatic channel. "The fact that all parties want to keep talking is itself significant," noted one diplomatic observer.

While Vice President J.D. Vance suggested Tehran might show flexibility on sensitive issues, Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi acknowledged prospects for further consultations. Critically, discussions have evolved beyond abstract questions about whether dialogue is possible to concrete debates over specific parameters—including timelines and formats for limiting Iran's uranium enrichment program, according to *The New York Times*. Though deep ideological divisions remain and the risk of escalation persists, the shift toward substantive bargaining over actual terms represents meaningful progress.

Pakistan's active mediation role reflects broader regional stakes: any expansion of conflict would threaten strategic and economic interests across South Asia. For now, both sides appear focused on exploring possibilities for temporary de-escalation rather than comprehensive settlement—a pragmatic approach that acknowledges current constraints while keeping diplomatic channels open for future progress.
