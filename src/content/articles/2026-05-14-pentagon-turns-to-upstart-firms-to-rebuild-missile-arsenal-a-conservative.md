The Pentagon has announced a major initiative to replenish America's weapons stockpiles, awarding contracts to emerging defense companies for more than 10,000 low-cost cruise missiles and up to 12,000 affordable hypersonic weapons. The move signals growing frustration with the traditional defense industry's cost overruns and sluggish production timelines.

The Department of Defense revealed framework agreements with four firms—Anduril, CoAspire, Leidos, and Zone 5—to launch the Low-Cost Containerized Missiles program. These contractors are expected to begin delivering test missiles in June. Anduril plans to supply its Barracuda-500M cruise missile, with at least 1,000 units scheduled over three years. In a separate deal, startup Castelion will provide at least 500 Blackbeard hypersonic missiles over two years, with potential expansion to 12,000 units over five years.

The initiative reflects lessons learned from recent conflicts that exposed weaknesses in America's defense manufacturing capacity. Years of providing military aid to Ukraine, combined with operations in the Middle East, consumed substantial quantities of critical munitions including Tomahawk cruise missiles and Precision Strike Missiles. Military planners have taken note of how inexpensive long-range attack systems have proven effective at a fraction of traditional missile costs.

By turning to venture-backed startups rather than established defense giants, Pentagon officials hope to achieve faster innovation and production. The shift represents a significant departure from the conventional procurement model, which has often been criticized for years-long development cycles, delays, and ballooning costs. Defense planners view this approach as essential for maintaining readiness in an era of great power competition, particularly regarding potential challenges in the Indo-Pacific region.
