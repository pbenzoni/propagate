Antigua and Barbuda has revised its constitutional oath of office to remove references to King Charles III and the British royal family, yet the Caribbean island nation stops short of full sovereignty—keeping the British monarch as its official head of state.

The twin-island nation gained independence from Britain in 1981 but chose to remain a constitutional monarchy within the Commonwealth. Under the new oath approved by Parliament late last year, cabinet members and elected officials now pledge allegiance to Antigua and Barbuda, its constitution, and its laws—not to a foreign monarch thousands of miles away.

The revised oath took effect following Prime Minister Gaston Browne's landslide reelection victory, with his Antigua and Barbuda Labour Party capturing 15 of 17 parliamentary seats. Browne called the election nearly two years early, citing global economic uncertainty, and voters delivered him a fourth consecutive term and more than a decade in power.

While fifteen Commonwealth nations still recognize the British monarch as sovereign, the trend appears to be shifting. Barbados severed ties with the Crown in 2021 to become a republic, though it remained in the Commonwealth. Even in Britain itself, support for the monarchy has declined to historic lows—just 51% in a 2025 survey, the weakest backing since records began in 1983.

For Antigua and Barbuda, the constitutional change represents a step toward self-governance, though critics may question why the nation maintains any formal ties to the British Crown at all if true independence is the goal.
