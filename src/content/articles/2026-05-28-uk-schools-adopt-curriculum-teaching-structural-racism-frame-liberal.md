A group of schools in Sheffield, England, has introduced teaching materials that distinguish between individual racial prejudice and systemic racism—a framework rooted in critical race theory that examines how power structures shape racial dynamics. The curriculum, adopted by Notre Dame High School and other institutions, teaches students that while Black individuals can hold racial prejudice against white people, racism in the UK context involves prejudice combined with structural power that predominantly benefits white communities.

The educational initiative aims to help students understand concepts like privilege and systemic inequality. Materials for younger students include lessons on "privilege," explaining that in Britain, white people are statistically less likely to experience racist bias, discrimination, or abuse. The curriculum encourages students with privilege to recognize inequality, challenge discriminatory language and behavior, and support those who face discrimination—an approach aligned with anti-racist education frameworks used in many progressive educational settings.

The teaching materials also prompt students to examine racial disparities in policing and criminal justice, asking them to consider why Black individuals are disproportionately stopped by police or incarcerated. These questions encourage critical thinking about whether such disparities reflect systemic bias or other factors—a pedagogical approach designed to foster analytical skills around complex social issues.

Conservative politicians have criticized the curriculum as "divisive identity politics." Shadow Education Secretary Laura Trott called the materials "deeply alarming" and urged government intervention to prevent their use. The debate reflects broader tensions in the UK and US over how schools should address historical and ongoing racial inequality, with progressive educators advocating for frank discussions of systemic racism while conservatives argue such frameworks unfairly characterize societal dynamics.
