A defense technology company valued at $61 billion is raising alarms among experts who warn that its founder's aggressive rhetoric about China and track record of failed weapons systems could push the U.S. toward a dangerous miscalculation in the Pacific.

Anduril Industries, founded less than a decade ago by Palmer Luckey, has secured more than $20 billion in Pentagon contracts for attack drones, autonomous aircraft, and AI-powered systems. Speaking at West Point and on popular podcasts, Luckey has repeatedly claimed that China will invade Taiwan by 2027—a timeline not supported by U.S. intelligence assessments. The 2026 Annual Threat Assessment explicitly states Beijing does "not currently plan to execute an invasion of Taiwan in 2027."

Yet Anduril's business model appears to depend on stoking fears of imminent conflict. During a speech at National Taiwan University, Luckey painted a dramatic scenario of a 2029 Chinese invasion thwarted by Anduril's weapons systems. The problem: those systems have repeatedly failed in real-world testing. Ukrainian forces stopped using Anduril's Altius drones in 2024 due to persistent malfunctions, while the company's Ghost reconnaissance drones proved vulnerable to jamming. During demonstrations for the U.S. military, unmanned attack boats running on Anduril's operating system refused commands and shut down.

Critics worry that overselling unproven technology while amplifying threat perceptions creates a dangerous feedback loop—one where miscalculation based on faulty assumptions and unreliable systems could trigger the very conflict the weapons are supposedly meant to prevent. As private defense contractors gain influence over military strategy, questions about accountability and the profit motive in threat assessment grow more urgent.
