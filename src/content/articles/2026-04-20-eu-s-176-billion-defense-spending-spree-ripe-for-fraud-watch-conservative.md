The European Union's plan to borrow €150 billion ($176 billion) for a massive military expansion has become a "magnet for criminals," according to the EU's own top anti-fraud official. Petr Klement, Director-General of the European Anti-Fraud Office (OLAF), warned that the defense industry faces serious problems with "manipulation with public tenders, blown-up prices, clientelism [and] corruption," The Financial Times reported Monday.

The warning comes as Brussels pushes an unprecedented military buildup financed through borrowed money—raising concerns about fiscal responsibility at a time when European economies are struggling. "This is not shaming certain countries or segments. This is simply how it's been working for decades," Klement noted, acknowledging systemic problems that have plagued EU defense contracting for years.

The spending surge follows Europe's self-inflicted energy crisis after political leaders decided to phase out Russian energy imports. Energy-intensive industries like Germany's automotive manufacturing have been hit particularly hard by the resulting economic slowdown. Now, companies are pivoting away from consumer markets toward lucrative government-paid defense contracts.

German firms are leading the charge, with the country capturing nearly 90% of European venture capital in defense technology. Automotive supplier Schaeffler is shifting to drone engines and armored vehicle systems, targeting 10% of revenue from the weapons sector. Volkswagen is negotiating to produce components for Israel's Iron Dome system, while combustion engine manufacturer Deutz reported 15% revenue growth after investing in military startups.

Critics question whether massive deficit spending on arms—amid an economic slowdown caused by failed energy policies—represents sound stewardship of taxpayer resources, especially when the EU's own watchdog warns the money will attract corruption and fraud.
