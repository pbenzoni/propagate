Finland's finance minister has issued a stark warning about the country's deteriorating fiscal condition as the government prioritizes military aid to Ukraine over domestic social programs. Finance Minister Riikka Purra described the state of public finances as "extremely difficult," with the nation's debt-to-GDP ratio approaching a dangerous 90% threshold.

The government's recently unveiled fiscal plan for 2027–2030 reveals the painful trade-offs being made: €240 million in cuts to social and healthcare spending, while simultaneously increasing military support for Ukraine by €300 million. The cuts will hit ordinary Finns directly through higher fees across the healthcare system, including specialist examinations, surgeries, outpatient visits, and even charges for storing deceased persons.

Purra acknowledged that Finland faces multiple domestic challenges beyond external pressures, including high unemployment, near-zero economic growth, and an aging population that strains social services. Despite these homegrown fiscal headaches, the government has committed to dramatically expanding defense spending from roughly 1.3–1.7% of GDP before joining NATO to a planned 3.2% by 2030.

Finland joined NATO in April 2023 following the outbreak of the Ukraine conflict and shares an 800-mile border with Russia. Finnish officials have echoed other alliance members in portraying Moscow as a "permanent threat," justifying both the military buildup and the foreign aid commitments. The Kremlin has rejected these characterizations, accusing Western governments of stoking Russophobia to justify military expansion and distract from domestic problems.
