A German court has ruled that calling Chancellor Friedrich Merz a "lying Fritz" constitutes criminal conduct warranting prosecution due to "particular public interest," imposing a fine exceeding €2,000—roughly equivalent to an average monthly salary—on the citizen who posted the comment.

The case represents just one of 39 criminal proceedings launched by German police targeting critical comments about Merz posted on Facebook. The prosecutions stem from an October incident when local police issued a drone no-fly zone warning ahead of Merz's visit, prompting online criticism that authorities characterized as insults. While some cases were dropped and others involving phrases like "Pinocchio" were not pursued, the court determined that "lying Fritz" could "incite further negative prejudices or aggression among like-minded individuals."

The government spokesman declined to comment on the prosecutions "out of respect for the judiciary," noting that Merz himself had not filed charges. The cases were brought under Article 188 of the German Criminal Code, which criminalizes insults against public political figures deemed likely to "significantly impede" their activities—a provision that civil liberties observers note grants considerable discretion to authorities in defining protected versus punishable speech.

The prosecutions come as Merz faces historic unpopularity, recently named the world's most unpopular political leader in public opinion surveys, with reports suggesting his own party has considered removing him due to record-low approval ratings. Free speech advocates have expressed mounting concern about the trajectory of expression rights in Germany, particularly following reports that regulators plan to require social media platforms to elevate government-approved outlets in their algorithms. Even the European Union stated in April that German authorities had misused hate-speech laws to restrict freedom of expression.
