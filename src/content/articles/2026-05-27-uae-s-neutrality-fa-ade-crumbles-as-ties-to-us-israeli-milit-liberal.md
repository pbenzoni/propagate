The United Arab Emirates has publicly positioned itself as neutral in the escalating conflict between Iran and a US-Israeli coalition, insisting it has not provided territory, airspace, or waters for military strikes. Yet mounting evidence suggests Abu Dhabi's careful balancing act between regional powers is collapsing under the weight of its deepening security partnerships with Washington and Tel Aviv.

According to multiple media reports, senior Israeli officials—including Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and intelligence chiefs—have made secret visits to the UAE during the current military escalation. CBS News reported Netanyahu met with Emirati President Mohammed bin Zayed in late March, while The Wall Street Journal documented at least two visits by Mossad chief David Barnea to coordinate operations. Though the UAE has denied some accounts, the pattern of high-level contacts has eroded its claims of neutrality in Iranian eyes.

The diplomatic crisis centers partly on a decades-old territorial dispute over three strategic islands near the Strait of Hormuz—Abu Musa, Greater Tunb, and Lesser Tunb—which Iran has controlled since 1971 but which the UAE claims as occupied territory. For Tehran, the Emirates' participation in the Abraham Accords with Israel and its role as a key US partner in the Persian Gulf have transformed it from neutral arbiter to perceived member of an "anti-Iranian coalition."

Human rights advocates and diplomacy experts have long warned that the region's complex web of military alliances risks drawing ostensibly neutral parties into widening conflicts. The UAE's predicament illustrates how authoritarian states' opaque security arrangements can undermine stated commitments to peace while increasing civilian populations' vulnerability to retaliation and escalation.
