European Union lawmakers and member states reached a preliminary agreement Monday on sweeping new deportation rules that human rights organizations warn could expose vulnerable asylum seekers to dangerous conditions and expanded government surveillance.

The regulations would dramatically extend detention periods for rejected asylum seekers from six months to two and a half years, while authorizing home searches, welfare cuts, and document confiscation. The deal also permits EU countries to transfer people to third countries if they cannot be returned to their nations of origin—a provision that has alarmed advocates who fear it could circumvent protections for those fleeing persecution.

Entry bans would double from five to ten years in most cases, with lifetime bans now possible under the framework. Rights groups and progressive lawmakers have raised serious concerns that the measures could normalize raids on immigrant communities and create detention conditions that violate international humanitarian standards.

The agreement still requires formal approval by EU governments and the European Parliament. Critics argue the rules represent a troubling shift away from Europe's commitments to asylum protections, even as proponents claim stronger enforcement is necessary. EU Migration Commissioner Magnus Brunner defended the deal, saying it would give the bloc "more control" over migration—language that echoes increasingly restrictive approaches across the continent.
