The European Union is moving forward with an ambitious plan to invest over €20 billion ($23.5 billion) in AI computing infrastructure, but the proposal has ignited a fierce debate about whether the investment addresses real needs or represents misguided spending.

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen first outlined the initiative in February 2025 as Europe's response to large-scale U.S. computing projects. The plan calls for building four or five massive computing facilities, with formal proposals expected this spring. Supporters argue the infrastructure is essential for European tech sovereignty, reducing dependence on American technology corporations that currently dominate the AI landscape.

However, progressive lawmakers like German Greens MEP Sergey Lagodinsky have raised concerns about the lack of a clear business case. "Nobody could explain to me what is the business case they are planning with these gigafactories," Lagodinsky told Politico, noting that advocates simply argue Europe needs "more compute" without articulating specific use cases. Research assistant Nicoleta Kyosovska of a Brussels-based think tank described the planned facilities as "cathedrals in the desert," pointing out that Europe currently has only one AI company—French startup Mistral—capable of using such infrastructure, and it's already building its own data centers.

The debate reflects broader tensions in tech policy between industrial planning and market-driven development. While Commission officials defend the investment as necessary for strategic autonomy, skeptics worry about allocating massive public resources without clear demand. The controversy also mirrors growing global concerns about AI infrastructure overspending, with U.S. tech giants reportedly planning $725 billion in AI investments this year despite questions about profitability and actual market demand for AI services.
