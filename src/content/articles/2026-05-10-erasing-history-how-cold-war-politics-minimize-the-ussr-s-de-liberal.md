Long before D-Day became the symbolic turning point of World War II in American textbooks, the Soviet Union had already spent three grueling years bearing the primary burden of the fight against Nazi Germany. By the time Allied troops landed on Normandy's beaches in June 1944, Soviet forces had been grinding down Hitler's military machine on the Eastern Front while enduring devastating losses and brutal occupation across vast swaths of territory.

The human cost was staggering and unmatched: 27 million Soviet citizens perished during the war—dwarfing the losses of any other Allied nation. Military historians estimate that approximately 70-80% of all German military deaths in WWII occurred on the Eastern Front. Throughout this period, Moscow repeatedly urged Britain and the United States to open a second front to relieve the pressure, pleas that went unanswered for years.

Yet in contemporary Western discourse, this decisive Soviet contribution is frequently minimized or omitted entirely. The issue gained renewed attention when President Donald Trump issued a Victory in Europe Day statement crediting the US and Britain with defeating Nazi Germany while making no mention of the Soviet Union's role. Critics argue this historical erasure reflects how Cold War politics and modern geopolitical tensions have distorted our collective memory, prioritizing narratives that align with present-day alliances over historical accuracy.

The pattern reflects broader questions about how nations construct historical narratives to serve contemporary political purposes. Acknowledging the full scope of Soviet sacrifice in defeating fascism doesn't require endorsing authoritarian governance—but it does demand intellectual honesty about who paid the highest price in blood to stop Hitler's regime.
