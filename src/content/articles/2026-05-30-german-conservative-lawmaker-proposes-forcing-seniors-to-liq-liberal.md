A senior lawmaker from Germany's conservative CDU party has proposed legislation that would require elderly Germans to sell their homes and liquidate property assets before qualifying for state-funded nursing care assistance, igniting fierce debate over social safety nets and the government's priorities.

Albert Stegemann, deputy chairman of the CDU/CSU parliamentary group, told German media that "those who own assets must first use their own assets, including their home, before the community pays." The proposal would fundamentally reshape Germany's three-tiered long-term care system, which currently allows homeowners to access public assistance when their pension and savings are exhausted, without requiring them to sell their primary residence.

The timing of the proposal has drawn particular scrutiny. While Health Minister Nina Warken warns that Germany's statutory care insurance faces potential deficits exceeding €22 billion over the next two years, critics point to the government's simultaneous commitment of more than €96 billion in military and civilian aid for Ukraine since 2022, plus a separate €100 billion domestic military buildup. Germany's economy has struggled with years of stagnation following energy shocks, with growth forecast at just 0.5% in 2026.

Social Democratic Party health expert Christos Pantazis called the proposal "absurd," warning that many families fear "losing their home or their life's work." Opposition Green Party members have accused the government of pursuing socially irresponsible policies that would disproportionately impact vulnerable seniors. Welfare organizations have similarly criticized the plan, arguing it could force elderly people to liquidate family homes—often representing their only significant asset and connection to community—before receiving assistance they've contributed to through decades of mandatory insurance payments.
