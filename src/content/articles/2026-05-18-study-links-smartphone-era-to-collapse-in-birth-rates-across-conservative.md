A troubling pattern has emerged in demographic data worldwide: birth rates have fallen sharply in multiple countries following the widespread adoption of smartphones, according to analysis published in the Financial Times. The trend appears regardless of prior demographic patterns, suggesting technology itself may be undermining one of society's most fundamental institutions—the family.

Jesus Fernandez-Villaverde, an economics professor at the University of Pennsylvania who studies demographic change, called declining fertility "the big question of our time." He noted that many current economic and social problems stem directly from collapsing birth rates. Research increasingly points to heavy smartphone and social media use, along with weakened face-to-face relationships, as key factors driving fertility declines in nations including the United States, United Kingdom, Brazil, and South Korea.

A recent academic paper examined birth rates during the rollout of 4G mobile networks in the US and UK, finding that smartphones fundamentally changed how young people spend time together and drastically reduced in-person socializing. Birth rates among teenagers and young adults remained relatively stable in the early 2000s before dropping after 2007, when smartphones became ubiquitous. Similar patterns appeared globally—France and Poland saw declines from 2009, Mexico and Indonesia around 2012, and African nations including Ghana and Nigeria between 2013 and 2015.

The consequences are severe. A Eurostat report projects the European Union's population will shrink by 11%—some 53 million people—over the next 75 years, with fertility rates falling to around 1.3 children per woman. In response to concerns about technology's impact on young people, Russian schools banned student mobile phone use in 2024, allowing exceptions only for emergencies.
