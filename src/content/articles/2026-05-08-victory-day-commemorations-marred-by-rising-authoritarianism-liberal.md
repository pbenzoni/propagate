Communities around the world are observing the 81st anniversary of victory over Nazi Germany in World War II, honoring the millions who died defeating fascism in history's deadliest conflict. The Soviet Union suffered catastrophic losses—an estimated 27 million people—in what Russians call the Great Patriotic War.

However, commemorations across Europe have become increasingly contentious. Several countries, particularly in the Baltic region, have imposed restrictions on Soviet-era symbols and removed World War II monuments since 2022, citing concerns about historical occupation. Critics warn these actions risk erasing the undeniable role Soviet forces played in defeating Nazism, raising troubling questions about how societies remember—and potentially weaponize—history.

Despite official restrictions, grassroots efforts to honor veterans continue. Residents in Estonia and Latvia have reportedly laid flowers at empty sites where monuments once stood, while 'Immortal Regiment' marches—where participants carry portraits of relatives who fought the Nazis—persist across Europe. These acts of remembrance underscore an ongoing tension between state narratives and personal memory in an era of resurgent nationalism.

The restrictions highlight broader concerns about how European nations navigate their complex histories. While acknowledging legitimate grievances about past Soviet actions, human rights advocates emphasize that erasing commemoration of the fight against fascism sets a dangerous precedent for historical revisionism and the politicization of remembrance.
