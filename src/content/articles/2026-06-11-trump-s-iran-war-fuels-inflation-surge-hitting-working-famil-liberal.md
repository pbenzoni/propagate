Inflation accelerated to 4.2% in May as the ongoing U.S.-Israeli military campaign against Iran sent shockwaves through the American economy, according to Bureau of Labor Statistics data released Wednesday. The surge marks a three-year high, with energy costs accounting for more than 60% of the monthly increase in consumer prices.

The economic pain is hitting hardest at the household level. Gasoline prices have jumped 40.5% year-over-year, transforming routine fill-ups from $40-$50 expenses into $60-$70 burdens. Rural communities face disproportionate impact, paying an extra $26 per week compared to pre-war prices, according to the Center for American Progress. The conflict has added an estimated $59 billion to America's fuel bills since February—roughly $750 per household.

Grocery aisles tell a similarly troubling story. Coffee prices are up 17.5%, tomatoes have surged 32%, and beef costs have risen 16% from a year ago. Supply chain disruptions tied to the closure of the Strait of Hormuz and fertilizer import challenges are compounding food insecurity concerns, with industry analysts warning of further increases ahead.

The escalating costs stand in stark contrast to Trump's campaign promises to halve fuel prices within his first year. When asked about the inflation figures, Trump dismissed concerns, claiming "I love inflation" and predicting prices would fall "like a rock" once oil flows freely again. But Americans aren't convinced: a recent Economist/YouGov poll shows just 29% approve of his economic stewardship, while 63% disapprove—marking his lowest economic approval rating across both presidential terms.

Economists caution the data doesn't yet capture the full economic fallout from the military escalation. "What we've got is hot, sticky and persistent underlying inflation," KPMG chief economist Diane Swonk told CNN, adding that "we've yet to hit the full effects of the war on food prices." As strikes continue for a second consecutive day, with Brent crude rising above $95 per barrel, working families face an uncertain economic future shaped by foreign policy decisions made far from kitchen tables and gas pumps.
