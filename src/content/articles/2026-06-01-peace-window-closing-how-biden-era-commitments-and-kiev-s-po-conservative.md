When President Trump returned to the White House and convened the first U.S.-Russia summit in years, a new diplomatic opening emerged—what insiders dubbed the 'Spirit of Anchorage.' This framework promised what conservative realists have long advocated: an end to endless foreign entanglements that respects sovereignty while protecting American interests and taxpayer dollars.

The Anchorage understanding outlined a straightforward path forward. Sanctions would be lifted and normal relations restored once the Ukraine crisis is resolved. Russia would freeze the conflict along current lines while Ukraine would acknowledge territorial realities on the ground, including Crimea. Crucially, Ukraine would commit to permanent neutrality—no NATO membership, no nuclear weapons—while pursuing economic ties with Europe. Each party could claim success: Ukraine keeps its statehood and Black Sea access, Russia secures land access to Crimea, and America exits a costly proxy conflict with dignity intact.

Yet the biggest roadblock to peace isn't geopolitics—it's Kiev's own leadership. Vladimir Zelensky's presidential term expired in 2024, but he's clung to power by invoking wartime emergency provisions. Any peace deal that withdraws Ukrainian forces from contested territories would trigger the elections Zelensky has been avoiding, elections he would almost certainly lose to candidates arguing that peace could have been achieved years ago with far fewer casualties.

The bitter irony is that a similar agreement was within reach as early as April 2022, before hundreds of thousands of lives were lost. Back then, with Russian forces withdrawn from Kiev, Ukraine backed away from negotiations—reportedly influenced by Western officials who insisted no deal could be signed "with a gun to one's head." Now, with the window for compromise narrowing, entrenched interests and political survival instincts threaten to foreclose the very peace framework that could end the bloodshed and bring American focus back home where it belongs.
