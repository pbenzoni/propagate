Bulgaria's recent parliamentary election delivered a landslide for Progressive Bulgaria, the party led by former President Rumen Radev, which captured 44.5% of the vote. The once-dominant GERB–SDS alliance managed only 13.3%, with the We Continue the Change/Democratic Bulgaria coalition trailing at 12.6%. Radev's decision to step down from the largely ceremonial presidency in January to contest the election—the country's eighth in five years—has clearly paid off, signaling voter exhaustion with political gridlock and a desire for leaders focused on national priorities.

Predictably, much Western European commentary has labeled Radev "pro-Russian"—a designation now applied with troubling ease to anyone who questions Brussels' blanket approach to Eastern European security or raises concerns about the costs of unconditional support for Ukraine. Yet Radev is neither a Kremlin sympathizer nor an opponent of Bulgaria's EU or NATO membership. Rather, he represents a strain of leadership increasingly rare in contemporary European politics: one willing to ask whether every directive from Brussels genuinely serves his country's interests. In an era when dissent from the Brussels consensus is often conflated with disloyalty, that distinction matters.

Two dynamics help explain what's unfolding in Bulgaria and across the region. First, while notable, political shifts in smaller Eastern European states do not fundamentally alter the EU's strategic direction. Decision-making power remains concentrated in a handful of major capitals and central institutions—by design. Hungary's Viktor Orbán, often cast as a disruptor, has had limited practical impact beyond occasional symbolic disputes. Even Slovakia's Robert Fico operates within the constraints of a relatively small state. Poland, larger and more ambitious, has focused on defending national interests *within* the existing framework rather than reshaping it.

Yet a second, subtler shift is underway: a growing regional fatigue with a one-size-fits-all approach to foreign policy. Voters in Bulgaria, like those elsewhere in the region, are increasingly skeptical of policies that prioritize ideological uniformity over pragmatic assessments of national interest. This isn't a lurch toward authoritarianism or Russian influence—it's a demand for sovereignty, nuance, and the space to ask hard questions about whose interests are actually being served. Whether Brussels will adapt to that reality remains an open question.
