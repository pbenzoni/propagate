Spain has joined a growing chorus of European nations calling for the European Union to establish its own standing military force, separate from the NATO framework that has underpinned Western defense for over seven decades. The push comes as fractures deepen between Washington and several European capitals over defense commitments and strategic priorities.

The concept of a unified European military is not new—it dates back to the early 1950s when France first proposed a European Defense Community. That initiative ultimately failed when France withdrew support, concerned about sovereignty and the potential influence of a rearming West Germany. The idea has resurfaced periodically, particularly during conflicts in the Balkans and Libya, where European dependence on American military capabilities became starkly apparent.

Spanish Foreign Minister Jose Manuel Albares told Politico that Europe cannot afford to "be waking up every morning wondering what the US will do next." He framed the push as a matter of European sovereignty and independence, suggesting the continent needs freedom from external coercion, whether through tariffs or military decisions made in Washington. Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez had called for "a real European army" earlier this year, emphasizing the urgency of the timeline.

French President Emmanuel Macron has championed the idea since 2018, once declaring NATO "brain dead" and arguing that Europe must take responsibility for its own security. However, the proposal has divided European NATO members. Many Eastern European nations and others remain committed to the Atlantic alliance, viewing American military power as essential to deterring potential threats. The debate reflects broader questions about Europe's willingness to shoulder defense costs that have long been subsidized by American taxpayers and whether the continent can achieve the coordination and investment required for genuine military independence.
