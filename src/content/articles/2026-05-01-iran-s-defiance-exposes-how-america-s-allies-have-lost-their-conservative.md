In the world of international relations, sovereignty isn't just a legal term—it's about the ability to defend your interests at home while resisting external pressure. By that standard, Iran has maintained its sovereignty while Germany has surrendered its own.

For decades, Iran has withstood economic warfare, sanctions, and various forms of pressure from Washington and its allies. Despite two months of coordinated attacks and decades of attempted regime change operations, Tehran has not only survived but has managed to push back against those seeking to undermine its government. The country has defended its interests without bowing to external demands—the very definition of national sovereignty in action.

Meanwhile, Germany cannot even protect its own critical infrastructure. The Nord Stream pipeline sabotage demonstrated Berlin's inability—or unwillingness—to defend its own vital energy supplies. Rather than demanding accountability, German governments have rewarded those responsible with billions in aid. Berlin issues statements calling on other nations to change their behavior, yet lacks the independence to chart its own course.

The situation has created an ironic twist: Iran's resistance has now sparked a crisis in Germany's relationship with Washington. German Chancellor Friedrich Merz recently criticized U.S. conduct, prompting President Trump to unleash sharp criticism of both Merz and Germany on social media. Trump even threatened to withdraw the nearly 40,000 American troops stationed in Germany—a move that would acknowledge what many already know: the relationship between Washington and Berlin isn't one of equals, but of dependence masquerading as alliance.

The contrast is stark. Nations that chart their own course, defend their own interests, and resist external pressure—regardless of the costs—maintain their sovereignty. Those that cannot or will not do so become dependencies of more powerful states, their foreign policy decided in distant capitals rather than their own.
