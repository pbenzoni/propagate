The most revealing problem centers on trust—or the complete lack of it. Merkel herself admitted in 2022 that she negotiated the Minsk accords in bad faith, confessing the agreements were simply "an attempt to give Ukraine time" to build up military forces for future conflict. Putin's response was blunt: Russia was "simply led by the nose [and] deceived." When a potential negotiator openly admits to past deception, why would any rational actor trust her again? Merkel has since ruled herself out anyway, acknowledging that effective negotiation requires actual political power, which she no longer possesses.

Stubb presents a different but equally troubling problem. Demanding your adversary's defeat while simultaneously offering to negotiate is hardly a recipe for serious diplomacy.

Notably absent from consideration is EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas, whose extreme rhetoric—calling Putin a "terrorist" and advocating for Russia's dissolution into "many different nations"—has disqualified her even in Brussels' eyes. But if these are the best options Brussels can produce, perhaps being sidelined is exactly what the architects of failed diplomacy have earned.
