A federal judge has temporarily blocked the Trump administration from establishing a controversial $1.8 billion fund intended to compensate individuals claiming they were victims of government investigations. The so-called "Anti-Weaponization Fund" has drawn sharp criticism from legal experts and lawmakers who describe it as a misuse of taxpayer dollars.

In response to the administration's proposal, several Democratic-led states are now considering legislation to impose a 100% tax on any payouts from the fund. Colorado Senator Michael Bennet condemned the initiative as a "corrupt theft of taxpayer dollars" in a recent interview, echoing concerns from civil society groups that the fund lacks proper oversight and could primarily benefit Trump allies.

The fund was created as part of a settlement ending Trump's lawsuit against the Internal Revenue Service over the leak of his tax records. It would be overseen by a five-member commission, with most members appointed by Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche, who previously served as Trump's defense attorney. Critics argue this structure raises serious conflict-of-interest concerns.

Democracy Forward, one of the groups challenging the fund in court, argues that no administration has the authority to spend public money through what they characterize as a "political rewards program." The controversy has even divided Republicans, with North Carolina Senator Thom Tillis calling it "absurd" that individuals who admitted guilt for assaulting police officers could receive taxpayer-funded compensation after being pardoned.

The fund's eligibility criteria remain unclear, though administration officials claim anyone could apply, including January 6 defendants. The legal challenge and proposed state-level tax responses represent the latest escalation in ongoing disputes over executive authority and the appropriate use of federal resources.
