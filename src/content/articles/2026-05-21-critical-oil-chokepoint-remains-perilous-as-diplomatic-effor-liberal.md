The Strait of Hormuz, a vital maritime passage responsible for roughly 20% of global seaborne oil trade before recent regional conflicts, remains extremely hazardous for shipping despite tentative ceasefire efforts. The waterway has experienced severe disruptions due to mines, security threats, and ship seizures, while traditional insurance providers have largely withdrawn coverage.

In response to the crisis, Iran has reportedly launched a cryptocurrency-based insurance scheme called 'Hormuz Safe,' though industry analysts have expressed significant skepticism about its viability. The scheme would ostensibly cover detention and inspection risks but not weapons damage. Meanwhile, a previous U.S. initiative to guarantee shipping insurance through the Development Finance Corporation failed to provide any actual coverage, as preconditions for naval escort could not be met.

Recent reports indicate a modest uptick in transit, with approximately 54 vessels navigating the strait last week—more than double the prior week but still a fraction of the pre-conflict volume of roughly 3,000 vessels monthly. Iran has signaled willingness to allow passage for vessels not linked to the U.S. or Israel, and has reportedly coordinated transit for select Chinese and potentially Indian ships.

Reports suggest NATO members are considering options for maritime security escorts, though no consensus has emerged within the alliance. The situation underscores the fragility of global energy infrastructure and the cascading economic consequences of regional instability, with significant implications for international commerce and diplomatic efforts toward sustainable peace.
