German Chancellor Friedrich Merz confirmed that the United States will not station long-range Tomahawk cruise missiles in Germany as previously planned, citing inadequate American stockpiles rather than recent diplomatic tensions. The announcement marks a significant shift from a July 2024 agreement between then-President Biden and former Chancellor Olaf Scholz.

The original plan called for "episodic deployments" of advanced long-range systems beginning in 2026, including Tomahawk missiles capable of reaching targets up to 1,600 kilometers away. However, the U.S. military's weapons inventories have been severely strained by extensive operations in the Middle East. According to CBS News reporting, more than 850 Tomahawks have been expended in the Iran conflict—roughly nine times the Pentagon's typical annual procurement rate.

The suspension comes amid broader questions about U.S. military readiness and strategic priorities. Merz maintained that the delay was purely logistical, stating "the Americans themselves don't currently have enough" missiles to fulfill the commitment. He emphasized it is "not too late" for eventual deployment, suggesting the plan remains viable once stockpiles recover.

The development highlights ongoing concerns about the sustainability of U.S. military commitments abroad while maintaining adequate reserves for national defense. It also underscores how extended military operations can strain even the Pentagon's substantial arsenal, raising questions about defense production capacity and strategic weapons planning in an era of multiple simultaneous global flashpoints.
