Bangladesh's government announced it will review a contentious trade agreement with the United States that critics argue severely compromises the nation's sovereignty and foreign policy independence. The decision follows massive protests led by progressive student organizations in the capital city of Dhaka.

The trade pact, signed in February by an unelected interim government just days before national elections, contains provisions that severely restrict Bangladesh's economic autonomy. According to reports, the agreement allows Washington to unilaterally terminate the deal if Bangladesh engages in trade with countries the US labels as "non-market" economies—a designation used for China and Russia. The pact also compels Bangladesh to automatically comply with US sanctions regimes, effectively stripping the nation of its right to maintain neutrality in global power conflicts.

Zahed Ur Rahman, an adviser to Prime Minister Tarique Rahman, acknowledged the concerns while noting potential implications for bilateral relations. "We can identify the areas that are more problematic and potentially harmful to the state, and conduct an initial review," he stated, signaling openness to renegotiating problematic provisions.

The agreement was rushed through by the interim government led by Nobel laureate Muhammad Yunus, raising questions about democratic legitimacy and the appropriateness of locking in long-term trade commitments during a transitional period. Progressive activists have characterized the deal as economic coercion that forces a developing nation to subordinate its interests to US geopolitical objectives. The pact's enforcement mechanisms—including the threat of reinstating higher tariffs if Bangladesh fails to comply—exemplify how trade agreements can be weaponized to constrain smaller nations' policy choices.
