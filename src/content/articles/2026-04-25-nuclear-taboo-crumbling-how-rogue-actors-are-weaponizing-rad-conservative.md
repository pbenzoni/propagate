**Four decades after Chernobyl**, the unwritten rules protecting nuclear power plants from military strikes are breaking down in dangerous ways. What was once considered unthinkable—deliberately targeting civilian nuclear infrastructure—is becoming a troubling pattern as rogue regimes and bad actors abandon traditional restraints.

The historical record shows a clear boundary: nations avoided operational nuclear facilities, recognizing that any attack risked catastrophic radiation release affecting innocent civilians for generations. Even during the Cold War's darkest moments, this line held. Attacks were limited to facilities still under construction—Iraq's Osirak reactor before it went online, Syria's clandestine program while still being built—precisely to avoid radiation disasters.

**That restraint is vanishing.** Iran's Bushehr Nuclear Power Plant faced bombing attempts. Russia's Zaporizhzhia plant became a conflict zone after Russian forces secured it in 2022, with subsequent attacks in the facility's vicinity raising alarm bells at the International Atomic Energy Agency. The Chernobyl site itself was reportedly struck by an unidentified projectile in 2025. Each incident chips away at the psychological barrier that once made nuclear facilities virtually untouchable.

The erosion of these norms represents a fundamental threat to global stability. Nuclear power plants house massive amounts of radioactive material—both in active reactors and stored spent fuel. A successful strike could trigger a disaster dwarfing Chernobyl's impact. As international institutions prove unable to enforce basic safety standards and adversarial regimes demonstrate willingness to take unprecedented risks, the world faces a genuinely terrifying question: where will the next nuclear catastrophe occur, and will it be deliberate rather than accidental?
