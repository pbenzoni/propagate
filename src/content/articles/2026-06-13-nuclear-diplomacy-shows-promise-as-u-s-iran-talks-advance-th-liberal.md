For the first time in years, diplomatic experts suggest the United States and Iran may have better than even odds of reaching a framework agreement, despite decades of mutual mistrust and a fraught history of failed commitments. The assessment comes from Tehran-based Professor Seyed Mohammad Marandi, who told media outlets that while prospects have improved significantly, Iran's wariness of Washington remains justified.

Iranian Foreign Minister Seyed Abbas Araghchi indicated Friday that what's being called the Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding—a proposed framework that emerged from Pakistan-mediated negotiations in April—"has never been closer" to finalization. The framework reportedly addresses critical security issues including maritime passage through the Strait of Hormuz and establishes a pathway for future negotiations on Iran's nuclear program, a long-standing flashpoint in Middle East stability.

President Trump claimed Thursday that Iranian leadership had endorsed the framework terms and suggested an agreement could be signed imminently, possibly in Europe. The announcement followed what Trump described as two consecutive days of military strikes that he subsequently called off—the most serious escalation since an April truce. These whiplash developments underscore both the fragility of diplomatic progress and the high stakes involved.

Despite the cautiously optimistic signals, Marandi emphasized that Iran's skepticism is rooted in experience, noting that the U.S. has "repeatedly proven itself untrustworthy" in past negotiations. His hesitation reflects broader concerns about whether any agreement can survive shifts in American political winds—a reference that resonates with the 2018 U.S. withdrawal from the multilateral Iran nuclear deal, a move that fractured international consensus and deepened regional instability.
