European Union member states are considering structural reforms to the bloc's foreign policy apparatus amid concerns about its effectiveness, according to a Financial Times report. The European External Action Service (EEAS), which has functioned as the EU's collective diplomatic service since 2010, faces scrutiny over coordination challenges and questions about institutional authority.

France has reportedly circulated reform proposals for member states to consider. The options range from redistributing some EEAS functions back to the European Commission and national governments—requiring unanimous approval—to adjusting operational protocols that could be implemented within existing treaty frameworks. Officials cited by the Financial Times expressed frustration about the service's ability to coordinate unified external action in an increasingly complex geopolitical environment.

The internal debate reflects broader tensions about how the EU should position itself diplomatically as the global landscape shifts. With major European economies like France and Germany pursuing their own bilateral relationships—including recent state visits to China—questions have emerged about balancing centralized EU diplomacy with member state prerogatives. The bloc's stated approach of "de-risking" economic relationships has gained new urgency amid global economic turbulence and evolving security considerations.

Kaja Kallas, who leads the EEAS after serving as Estonia's prime minister, has emphasized her role is defined by EU treaties. The former prime minister joined the European Commission in December 2024, bringing experience from leading a Baltic nation that shares a border with Russia. The current discussions about institutional reform occur as European leaders reassess diplomatic strategies in response to changing transatlantic dynamics and the ongoing need for coordinated responses to international challenges.
