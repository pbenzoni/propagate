Three Chinese astronauts successfully reached the Tiangong space station on Sunday, marking another milestone in Beijing's ambitious space program. The crew includes Hong Kong's first space traveler, former police officer Lai Ka-ying, whose participation underscores the mission's symbolic importance for regional integration.

Commander Zhu Yangzhu leads the six-month Shenzhou-23 mission, with one crew member planning to remain for a full year—China's longest continuous orbital stay. The mission focuses on scientific experiments and testing technologies that officials say will support future lunar exploration, with Beijing targeting a crewed Moon landing before 2030.

While Chinese officials insist they are "not competing" with other nations and frame lunar exploration as "a great feat for all of humanity," the reality is that international cooperation on space science has fractured along geopolitical lines. No humans have visited the Moon since NASA's 1972 Apollo 17 mission, and the U.S. Artemis program—which conducted a crewed flyby earlier this year—has largely excluded Chinese participation due to congressional restrictions on NASA collaboration with Beijing.

China has instead deepened space partnerships with Russia, announcing joint plans for a lunar research station and data-sharing on deep-space exploration. Critics argue that Cold War-style competition undermines the collaborative spirit that has historically driven major scientific breakthroughs, from the International Space Station to climate research. As humanity faces shared challenges from climate change to planetary defense, advocates for open science warn that siloed space programs may slow progress on discoveries that benefit all nations.
