Greece and Turkey—both longtime members of the Western military alliance—are locked in an escalating dispute over who controls key waters and islands in the Aegean Sea. What began as diplomatic disagreements has now moved into the realm of new laws, competing maps, and military posturing that threatens to turn allied nations into adversaries.

At the heart of the controversy is Turkey's Blue Homeland doctrine, a strategic framework developed by retired Turkish naval officers in the mid-2000s. The doctrine is straightforward: Turkey's national security depends not just on land borders, but on asserting sovereignty and economic rights over surrounding seas—the Aegean, the Black Sea, and the Eastern Mediterranean. Turkish officials argue they are simply defending legitimate maritime interests against a regional order imposed by outside powers. For a nation that sees itself constrained by treaties written by others, Blue Homeland represents a declaration of self-determination on the waves.

Greece sees it very differently. Athens views Turkey's doctrine as a direct challenge to settled international law and an attempt to chip away at Greek sovereignty over hundreds of islands and islets. The fear in Greece is that Turkey is deliberately creating legal ambiguity where none exists, laying the groundwork for future pressure or even military confrontation. Memories of the 1996 Imia crisis—when the two nations nearly went to war over uninhabited rocks—still shape how both sides view each new provocation.

The timing couldn't be worse for NATO. With the alliance already stretched by global commitments and internal divisions, the last thing it needs is two member states on the brink of conflict with each other. Yet that is exactly the risk as Turkey moves forward with new maritime jurisdiction laws and Greece vows to reject any unilateral claims.
