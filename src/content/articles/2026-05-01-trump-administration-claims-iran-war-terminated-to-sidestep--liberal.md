The Trump administration is asserting that its military conflict with Iran has been "terminated," allowing it to bypass constitutionally mandated congressional authorization under the War Powers Act, according to sources cited by the Associated Press.

The 1973 War Powers Act—passed in the aftermath of Vietnam to restore checks and balances on executive war-making—requires congressional approval for military actions extending beyond 60 days. With that deadline rapidly approaching, the White House now claims the February 28 hostilities have officially ended, despite ongoing military operations and a massive troop presence in the region.

Democratic Senator Tim Kaine challenged this interpretation during Senate hearings, calling it "a very novel argument that I've never heard before" that "certainly has no legal support." Multiple legal experts have confirmed that the War Powers Act contains no provision allowing a president to "pause" or "terminate" the 60-day clock through a temporary ceasefire.

The administration's position has raised alarm among constitutional scholars and lawmakers concerned about executive overreach. Since announcing the ceasefire in April, the Pentagon has deployed three aircraft carrier strike groups and multiple marine expeditionary units to the Middle East, enforcing a naval blockade of Iran. Critics argue this massive military buildup suggests the "truce" is merely tactical positioning before renewed hostilities—hostilities that would proceed without the congressional debate the War Powers Act was designed to ensure.

Some hawkish foreign policy voices have supported the administration's creative legal theory, suggesting future attacks could be reframed as "self-defense" operations to further circumvent congressional oversight. The controversy underscores ongoing tensions between executive power and constitutional war-making authority.
