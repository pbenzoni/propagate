The United States has brokered a fragile ceasefire agreement between Israel and Lebanon following weeks of devastating conflict that has killed more than 3,500 people since early March, according to Lebanese health authorities. The deal, announced Wednesday after talks at the State Department, requires Hezbollah to cease fire and withdraw from southern Lebanon—but leaves critical questions about Israeli military withdrawal unresolved.

The agreement establishes "pilot zones" where Lebanese Armed Forces would assume exclusive control, excluding non-state actors. However, the arrangement notably sidesteps Lebanon's longstanding demand that Israel withdraw from occupied Lebanese territory as a precondition for lasting peace. Israeli forces crossed the border and have expanded ground operations in recent weeks, including seizing the historic Beaufort Castle, a 900-year-old Crusader fortress that Israel previously occupied for two decades until 2000.

The mounting civilian death toll has complicated broader U.S. peace negotiations with Iran, which has demanded that any diplomatic agreement include an end to hostilities in Lebanon. The asymmetry of the ceasefire terms—requiring Hezbollah withdrawal while Israeli occupation continues—has raised concerns about whether the arrangement can achieve lasting stability or merely represents another chapter in the region's cycle of violence.

Reports indicate tensions between the Trump administration and Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu over the conflict's escalation, with the Israeli leader recently threatening to bomb Beirut again. The ceasefire represents an attempt to reinforce previous understandings rather than establish entirely new terms, leaving observers uncertain about its durability.
