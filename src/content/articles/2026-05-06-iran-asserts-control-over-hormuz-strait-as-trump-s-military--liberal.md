Iran has established a new regulatory framework for maritime traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, a critical chokepoint for global energy supplies, as diplomatic efforts struggle to resolve a dangerous standoff with the United States. The Persian Gulf Strait Authority will now email transit rules to vessels seeking passage, requiring compliance before permits are granted.

The development follows President Trump's abrupt pause of "Project Freedom"—a military escort operation launched just days earlier—after it immediately escalated tensions. Iranian Foreign Minister Seyed Abbas Araghchi warned that the military approach represents "Project Deadlock," urging focus on Pakistani-mediated peace talks instead of armed confrontation that risks dragging the region "back into quagmire."

The strait, which carries approximately 20% of the world's seaborne oil and liquified natural gas, has been effectively disrupted since late February following U.S. and Israeli strikes against Iran. Iranian forces have blocked vessels linked to the U.S., Israel, and their allies, while Washington has maintained a blockade of Iranian ports—an action Tehran characterizes as violating a fragile ceasefire and obstructing lasting peace.

Draft Iranian legislation would reportedly ban Israeli-linked ships entirely, impose strict limitations on U.S.-connected vessels, and levy transit fees on ships from non-hostile nations. The closure has already pushed oil prices to multi-year highs, with energy executives warning that physical shortages could materialize within weeks, potentially rivaling the supply crisis of the 1970s. Despite Trump's claims of "tremendous military success," the situation underscores the limits of military solutions to complex geopolitical disputes.
