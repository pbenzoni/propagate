A debate over Indigenous rights and the restoration of original place names in Canada has reignited questions about historical injustices and property rights that were never properly resolved through legal agreements. Hereditary Chief Phil Lane Jr., a citizen of the Chickasaw and Ihanktonwan Nations, argues that restoring native place names represents a modest step toward acknowledging historical truth rather than an assault on Canadian identity.

The crux of the issue centers on a legal and historical reality: over 95% of British Columbia remains unceded territory—land that was never surrendered through formal treaty. When British Columbia joined Canada in 1871, the provincial government declined to recognize Aboriginal Title or negotiate treaties across most of the province. According to Chief Lane, colonial governments seized these territories after devastating disease epidemics weakened Indigenous communities, assuming native populations would disappear entirely. This seizure occurred without legal treaties or consent from the original inhabitants.

The human cost of colonization was catastrophic. Indigenous oral traditions indicate that more than one million people lived in what is now British Columbia before European contact. Introduced diseases like smallpox decimated entire communities, reducing the Indigenous population to approximately 40,000. Some nations saw their populations collapse by over 99%—the Nuxalk Nation fell from over 30,000 to around 300, while the Tsleil-Waututh Nation near Vancouver was reduced from over 10,000 to fewer than 20 people.

Chief Lane points to the church-operated, government-funded residential school system as a central instrument of cultural destruction. For over a century, more than 150,000 Indigenous children were forcibly removed from their families. Justice Murray Sinclair, who chaired Canada's Truth and Reconciliation Commission, estimates that 25,000 or more children never returned home. The 2021 discovery of over 200 unmarked graves at the former Kamloops Indian Residential School brought renewed attention to this dark chapter.

The recent formal recognition of Haida Aboriginal Title across all of Haida Gwaii—covering 10,180 square kilometers—by both British Columbia and Canada confirms what Indigenous peoples have long maintained: their titles were never lawfully extinguished. Chief Lane argues that restoring native place names is not about erasing Canadian heritage but about acknowledging the original inhabitants and honoring promises that were either broken or never made. For communities that lost their lands, languages, and thousands of children to policies they never consented to, the return of traditional place names represents a basic form of recognition and respect.
