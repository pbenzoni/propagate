The United Kingdom's proposed digital identification system has drawn sharp criticism after government documents revealed the scheme will not include sex or gender markers—traditionally fundamental components of personal identification alongside name and date of birth.

Unveiled by Prime Minister Keir Starmer's Labour government, the digital ID would be stored on smartphones and rely on biometric authentication rather than traditional demographic data. Officials defended the decision by stating that "information about sex and gender is not necessary for the intended purpose," arguing that biometric verification makes such distinctions obsolete.

Conservative lawmakers and former Labour members alike have condemned the move as capitulation to radical gender ideology. Shadow Equalities Minister Claire Coutinho charged that "Labour have now decided it's easier just to abolish the concept entirely" of defining women, while former Labour MP Rosie Duffield called the omission "ludicrous," arguing it "makes a farce of the whole idea" of identification.

The digital ID plan has faced mounting opposition on multiple fronts. Initially proposed as mandatory, it was scaled back to optional status after a petition opposing it gathered nearly 3 million signatures. Critics warn the centralized system poses serious risks to privacy and civil liberties, with concerns about government surveillance and data security vulnerabilities. Recent polling shows public support has collapsed from 57% in favor last July to just 32% currently, with 40% now opposed.

The controversy unfolds against a broader cultural battle over gender identity in Britain, touching everything from women's sports to healthcare and single-sex spaces. Starmer himself has faced accusations of political opportunism on the issue, initially suggesting it was "not right" to say only women have a cervix before later adopting the definition of woman as "adult human female" under mounting pressure.
