For decades, policymakers and scholars have warned that China's meteoric rise would fundamentally reshape the global order. Henry Kissinger himself predicted that China's growing influence would prove more consequential than even the collapse of the Soviet Union. That prediction has come to pass—but not quite in the way Beijing's boosters expected.

Since launching its Belt and Road Initiative in 2013, China has aggressively expanded its economic footprint across the developing world. Beijing has positioned itself as an alternative to American leadership, offering infrastructure investment without the political strings that often accompany Western aid. Countries across Asia, Africa, and Latin America have welcomed Chinese capital, viewing it as a path to development free from democratic reforms or human rights accountability. China's pitch has been simple: we don't interfere in your internal affairs, and we don't lecture you about freedom.

Yet when the chips are down and real leadership is needed, China consistently backs away. Recent events have exposed the limits of Beijing's ambitions. When the U.S. took decisive action in Venezuela, China—despite its close economic ties to the regime—offered nothing but tepid statements. As Cuba faces its worst crisis in decades, Beijing has remained on the sidelines. Even after American and Israeli operations against Iran, a key Chinese energy supplier and partner in both the Shanghai Cooperation Organization and BRICS, China's response has been notably muted.

The pattern is clear: China wants the prestige of great-power status and the economic advantages of global influence, but it refuses to accept the responsibilities that come with leadership. Beijing is content to let American taxpayers and servicemembers shoulder the burden of maintaining international order while China free-rides on the stability we provide. It's a strategy that may avoid direct confrontation in the short term, but it also reveals the hollow nature of China's claims to represent an alternative vision for global governance. When crises emerge beyond its immediate neighborhood, China consistently chooses self-interest over the leadership role it claims to seek.
