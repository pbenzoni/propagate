The Pentagon has revised its estimate of the Iran war's cost to $29 billion, according to testimony from acting Defense Department Comptroller Jules Hurst before the House Appropriations Committee this week. The figure represents an increase from the $25 billion estimate Hurst provided to Congress in late April, with most costs attributed to depleted munition stockpiles.

However, independent analysis paints a dramatically different fiscal picture. According to journalist Stephen Semler, co-founder of the Security Policy Reform Institute, the war's actual cost reached approximately $71.8 billion in just the first 60 days—nearly two and a half times the Pentagon's current estimate. Semler's analysis, published in Popular Information and based on military procurement data and official statements, found that expended munitions alone accounted for more than $41 billion, with military operations costs and damage to U.S. regional assets comprising most of the remainder.

The discrepancy raises questions about transparency and accountability in defense spending oversight. The Center for Strategic and International Studies estimates the conflict has forced the U.S. to deplete between 50% and 80% of critical interceptor stockpiles, including THAAD missiles described as "the most critical due to their low inventory and lack of alternatives."

Meanwhile, according to U.S. intelligence estimates cited by the New York Times, Iran has retained approximately 70% of its mobile launchers and pre-war missile stockpile, contradicting claims by President Trump that Iranian capabilities were largely destroyed. The Times reports Iran has also restored 30 of 33 coastal missile sites along the strategically vital Strait of Hormuz, where tensions remain high as Washington maintains a blockade of Iranian ports with neither side yielding in negotiations.
