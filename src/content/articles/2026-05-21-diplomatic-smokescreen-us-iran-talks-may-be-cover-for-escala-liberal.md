As the world watches anxiously, what are being framed as peace negotiations between the United States and Iran may actually be laying groundwork for renewed military confrontation rather than diplomatic resolution. Despite public statements about pursuing a deal and ongoing shuttle diplomacy through intermediaries in Pakistan, Qatar, and Turkey, recent developments suggest both nations are entrenching in fundamentally incompatible positions.

Recent talks in Islamabad failed to produce any breakthrough, with core disputes over the Strait of Hormuz and Iran's nuclear program remaining unresolved. Alarmingly, President Trump recently revealed he had planned strikes on Iran for May 19 but postponed them at the request of Gulf allies—a statement that underscores how close the region remains to renewed violence.

Many progressives had hoped that growing American fatigue with Middle Eastern wars and increasing criticism of unconditional support for Israel might push the administration toward genuine diplomacy. There were also reasonable expectations that Trump's desire to maintain his image as someone who ends conflicts rather than escalating them might motivate restraint. Yet the terms now being exchanged between Washington and Tehran suggest something darker: Iran is reportedly demanding compensation for previous US strikes and recognition of its control over the Strait of Hormuz—demands Washington views as geopolitical capitulation.

When parties truly want peace, they create space for compromise and negotiate tradeoffs. But when ultimatums are designed to be unacceptable, diplomacy becomes theater. Both sides appear to be using this pause not to craft durable agreements but to regroup, assess damage, and prepare for the next round of hostilities. The diplomatic channel remains open primarily to justify continued ultimatums while keeping military options ready—a dangerous dynamic that prioritizes posturing over the difficult work of preventing war.
