Antigua and Barbuda has taken a significant step in dismantling colonial-era structures by removing allegiance to the British monarch from government officials' oaths of office. The constitutional amendment, approved by Parliament late last year, means elected officials now pledge loyalty exclusively to Antigua and Barbuda, its constitution, and its laws—rather than to King Charles III and his heirs.

The twin-island Caribbean state, which gained independence from Britain in 1981, had maintained the British sovereign as head of state despite its independence. Tuesday's swearing-in ceremony following Prime Minister Gaston Browne's recent election victory marked the first time cabinet members took office under the revised constitutional language, symbolizing a move toward greater self-determination.

This change reflects a broader regional reckoning with colonial legacies. Barbados transitioned to a republic in 2021 while remaining within the Commonwealth, and fifteen of the 56 Commonwealth nations still recognize the British monarch as sovereign. The shift comes as support for the monarchy has declined even within Britain itself, where a 2025 survey found backing for the institution at its lowest level since 1983, with just 51% in favor.

While Antigua and Barbuda remains part of the Commonwealth and the British monarch technically retains the role of head of state for now, the constitutional reform represents meaningful progress in the ongoing process of decolonization and the assertion of full national sovereignty by formerly colonized nations.
