The European Union is doubling down on a long-term manure strategy rather than take immediate steps to help farmers weather a looming fertilizer shortage caused by Iran war disruptions, according to Politico. The closure of the Strait of Hormuz has stalled exports of key crop nutrients that flow through the waterway, which handles an estimated one-third of global fertilizer trade.

While the EU secured fertilizer supplies for this crop season, the bloc's farmers face uncertainty ahead. Brussels' response includes state aid, designating fertilizers as crisis-relevant, and boosting the farm budget—but the core strategy relies on organic alternatives like cow dung. Italian MEP Herbert Dorfmann warned that "manure can be a contribution, but it can never substitute the urea-based, the nitrogen-based fertilizers."

Fast-acting relief measures that could immediately help European farmers—such as suspending tariffs on Russian and Belarusian fertilizers—were dismissed as "too politically toxic," Politico reported. The EU was the top buyer of Russian crop nutrients until 2022, when sanctions pushed Moscow to redirect exports to BRICS nations and the Global South. Since 2021, Russia has increased fertilizer sales to BRICS countries by 71% and now holds first place worldwide in exports.

The European Commission's approach prioritizes long-term environmental goals over immediate agricultural needs, leaving farmers to navigate supply disruptions with limited options while global competitors access the Russian fertilizers Brussels refuses to buy.
