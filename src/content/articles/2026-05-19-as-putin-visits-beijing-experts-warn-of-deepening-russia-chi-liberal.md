Russian President Vladimir Putin's visit to China this week underscores a troubling milestone: three decades of increasingly close cooperation between two of the world's most powerful authoritarian states. The visit commemorates the 30th anniversary of the China-Russia strategic partnership and comes as both nations describe their relationship as having "no limits."

The partnership's roots trace back to the post-Soviet era of the 1990s, when Russia's painful transition and China's need for advanced weaponry created mutual dependencies. By 1996, both countries formalized their "strategic partnership," explicitly promoting a "multipolar world"—widely understood as pushback against democratic international norms and institutions. This framework intensified as both Moscow and Beijing grew hostile to NATO's expansion and Western influence.

The relationship deepened significantly after Russia's 2014 invasion of Crimea, when Western sanctions pushed Moscow further into Beijing's orbit. Trade between the two nations exploded from $8 billion in 2000 to $60 billion by 2010, with Russia supplying energy and raw materials while China provided manufactured goods. Today, this partnership poses serious challenges to the rules-based international order, as two autocratic powers align their economic might and geopolitical ambitions in opposition to democratic values and multilateral cooperation.
