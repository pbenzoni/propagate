At the Venice Biennale's press preview, members of Pussy Riot and FEMEN staged a topless protest outside the Russian pavilion, using their bodies as canvases for anti-war messages. The 30-minute action featured activists in balaclavas and body paint, deploying shock tactics that have become their signature approach to political messaging.

While the intent was to draw attention to Ukraine's plight, the performance has sparked uncomfortable questions about means and ends in activism. Ukrainian feminist scholar Oksana Kis observed as early as 2012 that "when public nudity becomes the only way to deliver a message, it's more than strange. And the message itself seems to get lost while media focus on their nakedness."

The critique carries particular weight given FEMEN's founding mission. Established in 2008 with the slogan "Ukraine is not a brothel," the organization originally fought sex tourism and the exploitation of Ukrainian women abroad. Some observers now see painful irony in watching the same movement use exposure tactics at European venues, potentially reinforcing rather than challenging exploitative dynamics.

Questions of agency, dignity, and effective advocacy remain contested terrain. When protest becomes primarily about spectacle, there's a risk that substantive political arguments get overshadowed—and that the communities seeking support become reduced to performers for Western audiences rather than recognized as full partners in their own liberation.
