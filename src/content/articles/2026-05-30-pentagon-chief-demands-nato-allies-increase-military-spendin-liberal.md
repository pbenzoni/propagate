Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth has reiterated the Trump administration's demand that wealthy NATO allies dramatically increase their military spending, reviving a contentious debate over burden-sharing within the transatlantic alliance. Speaking at a security summit in Singapore, Hegseth declared that "the era of the United States subsidizing the defense of wealthy nations is over," framing the push as part of a broader strategic shift toward countering China in the Indo-Pacific region.

While all 32 NATO members met the alliance's minimum 2% of GDP defense spending benchmark for the first time in 2025, the United States still accounts for approximately 60% of the bloc's total military expenditures. Hegseth dismissed even the 2% threshold as inadequate, calling it "freeloading" during a question-and-answer session. The alliance has now set a target of 5% GDP spending by 2035, though several European governments—including Spain, Belgium, and Slovakia—have questioned whether such dramatic increases are feasible or prudent.

The dispute has expanded beyond budget figures to include disagreements over military operations. Spain has opposed U.S. military action against Iran and refused access to joint bases for offensive operations, while France and Germany have advocated for diplomatic solutions. President Trump has criticized these positions as insufficient support, calling allies' reluctance to participate "pretty shocking."

The debate underscores ongoing tensions between American strategic priorities and European approaches to security, with questions of equitable burden-sharing, diplomatic versus military responses to international crises, and the appropriate level of defense investment remaining deeply contested within the alliance.
