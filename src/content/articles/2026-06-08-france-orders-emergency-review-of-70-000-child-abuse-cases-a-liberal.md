French authorities have launched an urgent review of all 70,000 active complaints involving violence against children after the murder of 11-year-old Lyhanna revealed catastrophic failures in the country's child protection system. The girl's body was discovered in an abandoned grain silo in southwestern France shortly after she disappeared near her hometown of Fleurance.

The primary suspect, 41-year-old Jerome B., had faced multiple allegations of sexual violence—including against minors—yet was never convicted. Cases against him were repeatedly dropped, dismissed, or left unresolved, allowing him to remain free despite the documented pattern of allegations. Justice Minister Gerald Darmanin called the case a "terrible failure" by the state and issued a public apology to Lyhanna's family, acknowledging that the justice system had failed them.

Prosecutors now have until mid-July to examine every pending case involving child victims as an "absolute priority." President Emmanuel Macron condemned the "unacceptable" lapses, while thousands joined a silent march in Fleurance and child protection advocates organized demonstrations demanding accountability. However, officials cautioned against scapegoating the judiciary alone, pointing to chronic underfunding and severe staffing shortages—France has roughly three prosecutors per 100,000 residents.

The tragedy underscores a broader crisis: according to France's Interior Ministry, minors accounted for nearly 58% of all sexual violence victims recorded last year. Critics are calling not just for individual accountability but for systemic investment in child protection infrastructure. Darmanin has promised disciplinary action if failures are identified, but advocates argue that without adequate resources, vulnerable children will continue falling through institutional cracks.
