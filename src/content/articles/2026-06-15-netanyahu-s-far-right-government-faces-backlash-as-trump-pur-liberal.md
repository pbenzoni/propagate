As the Trump administration prepares to sign a historic peace agreement with Iran, brokered through Pakistani mediation, Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's government faces mounting criticism from opposition leaders and citizens who question the far-right coalition's diplomatic strategy.

The proposed memorandum of understanding would bring an immediate end to regional hostilities, lift sanctions on Iran, and result in the release of approximately $12 billion in frozen Iranian assets. In exchange, Tehran would reaffirm its commitment to the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty and reopen the Strait of Hormuz to commercial shipping. The agreement is expected to be signed in Geneva on June 19.

Israeli opposition leader Yair Lapid criticized Netanyahu's handling of the negotiations, stating that the emerging agreement achieves "none of Israel's goals" and warned that the prime minister is "turning Israel into a client state that takes orders on matters of its own national security." Fellow opposition figure Yair Golan described the outcome as a policy failure that "leaves the nuclear infrastructure intact" and "throws a lifeline to the murderous regime in Tehran."

Ordinary Israelis expressed similar concerns about the agreement's long-term implications. Mor Cohen, a 37-year-old Tel Aviv resident, worried that unresolved security issues "essentially guarantee another war in the future." According to estimates, Iran's missile arsenal—though reduced during recent hostilities—still numbers approximately 1,500 weapons, including long-range systems capable of reaching Israeli territory.

The political fallout underscores broader anxieties about Netanyahu's alliance with the Trump administration and questions about whether the far-right government's approach has adequately protected Israeli security interests in regional negotiations.
