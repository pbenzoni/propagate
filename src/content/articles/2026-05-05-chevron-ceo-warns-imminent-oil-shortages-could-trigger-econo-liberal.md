The ongoing conflict in the Middle East and the continued closure of the Strait of Hormuz could trigger widespread oil shortages within weeks, according to Chevron CEO Mike Wirth, raising urgent questions about energy security and the environmental costs of fossil fuel dependence.

Speaking at the Milken Institute Global Conference, Wirth warned that physical availability—not just price—will soon become the primary concern as the critical shipping lane remains blocked. The strait typically carries about a fifth of global seaborne oil and liquefied natural gas. "We will start to see physical shortages," he said, noting that economies in Asia and Europe would be hit first and hardest as supplies tighten and strategic reserves are depleted.

The crisis has already begun affecting consumers and businesses worldwide. European airlines are restricting jet fuel use and cutting flights, while several Asian countries have implemented demand-reduction measures. Spirit Airlines cited surging fuel costs as a factor in its decision to cease operations. The International Energy Agency has called the disruptions "the biggest energy security threat in history," with approximately 13 million barrels per day of oil supplies disrupted.

Wirth compared the potential impact to the oil crises of the 1970s, which caused widespread fuel shortages and economic disruption across industrialized nations. The World Bank has projected energy prices could jump 24% this year, with broader commodity costs rising 16%. While the United States, as a net crude exporter, may be less immediately affected than other regions, Americans will still face higher prices at the pump and throughout the supply chain, underscoring the interconnected nature of global energy markets and the vulnerability created by continued reliance on fossil fuels.
