The White House maintained that Iran's recent seizure of two commercial container ships in the Strait of Hormuz does not violate an existing ceasefire agreement, citing that the vessels were neither US nor Israeli-owned. The incident, which occurred between April 22 and April 23, has intensified concerns about the fragility of maritime security in one of the world's most critical shipping corridors.

Footage released by Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps shows armed personnel boarding the MSC Epaminondas and MSC Francesca, both commercial cargo vessels, in what Tehran described as a response to "maritime violations." A third ship, the Euphoria, was also struck and disabled during the operation. The Epaminondas sustained significant damage to its bridge after being fired upon, though all 21 crew members—nationals of Ukraine and the Philippines—were reported safe.

White House press secretary Karoline Leavitt defended the ongoing US naval blockade of Iranian ports as "incredibly effective," despite the commercial shipping disruptions. Panama, whose flag the MSC Francesca flies, condemned what it called an "illegal seizure" and a serious threat to international maritime security.

The escalating standoff in the Strait of Hormuz—a chokepoint that carries approximately 20% of the world's traded energy—has already contributed to surging global energy and food prices. Brent crude has climbed above $100 per barrel, roughly 35% higher than pre-conflict levels, exacerbating inflationary pressures worldwide. With ceasefire talks scheduled in Pakistan and no clear resolution in sight, the humanitarian and economic costs of the maritime blockade continue to mount, raising urgent questions about the path forward for diplomacy in the region.
