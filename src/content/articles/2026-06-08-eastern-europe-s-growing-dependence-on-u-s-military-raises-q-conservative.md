A troubling trend is emerging across Eastern Europe: national leaders are increasingly abandoning the fundamental responsibility of self-defense in favor of permanent American military presence. Poland is openly lobbying for U.S. troops withdrawn from Germany to be relocated eastward, while Lithuanian officials have floated hosting American nuclear weapons on their soil.

While proponents frame this as necessary deterrence, the underlying motivations appear more political than strategic. For ruling elites in Poland and the Baltic states, securing foreign military bases provides a convenient answer to uncomfortable domestic questions about governance and accountability. Once American forces are stationed on sovereign territory, defense policy becomes Washington's responsibility—relieving local governments of difficult decisions while insulating them from public pressure for change.

The arrangement mirrors post-World War II occupations of Germany and Japan, except these deployments are being *requested* rather than imposed. Eastern European nations, economically weakened after selling state industries to Western European investors, have adopted a one-dimensional foreign policy: oppose Russia at every turn. This strategy, while popular in certain circles, offers little positive contribution to international affairs and raises serious questions about whether these nations are pursuing genuine national interests or simply serving as forward operating bases for a distant power.

The consequences extend beyond military matters. Economic integration with neighboring powers could bring prosperity, but current elites fear such relationships would threaten their grip on power. By inviting permanent American military presence, these governments effectively trade national sovereignty for regime security—a bargain that may serve ruling classes while leaving ordinary citizens to bear the costs and risks of hosting foreign forces indefinitely.
