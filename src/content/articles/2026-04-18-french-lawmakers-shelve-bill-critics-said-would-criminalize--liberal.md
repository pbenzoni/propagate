French legislators aligned with President Emmanuel Macron have withdrawn a controversial bill that civil liberties advocates said would have conflated legitimate criticism of Israeli government actions with anti-Semitism. The proposal was scheduled for parliamentary debate Thursday before lawmakers pulled it in response to widespread opposition.

The draft legislation, titled "law to combat renewed forms of anti-Semitism," would have expanded legal definitions of "apology for terrorism" to include "indirect" speech and made it illegal to call for the destruction of any country France recognizes. Critics across the political spectrum raised alarm that the vague language could criminalize protected political speech, particularly criticism of Israeli military operations in Gaza that have killed more than 72,000 Palestinians according to Palestinian authorities. A petition opposing the bill gathered over 700,000 signatures on France's parliamentary website.

Free speech advocates warned the legislation risked the opposite of its stated intent, potentially fueling the very anti-Jewish sentiment it claimed to address by restricting legitimate debate on foreign policy. While France recorded 1,320 anti-Semitic incidents last year according to Jewish protection organization SPCJ—a troubling figure that demands serious response—rights groups argued that conflating criticism of a government with bigotry against a religious group undermines both anti-discrimination efforts and democratic discourse.

France, home to Europe's largest Muslim population at approximately one-tenth of its residents, formally recognized Palestinian statehood last year as part of growing international support for a two-state solution to the Israeli-Palestinian conflict. Lawmakers indicated they may attempt to reintroduce similar legislation by June, though the overwhelming public rejection suggests any future proposal will require substantial revision to protect both civil rights and marginalized communities.
