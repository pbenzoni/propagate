Former President Donald Trump's recent visit to China was primarily an attempt to mitigate economic fallout from the administration's Iran policy, according to geopolitical analyst Danny Haiphong. The analyst argues that Trump arrived in Beijing from a "far weaker position" than China, seeking deals to stabilize an economy destabilized by the administration's own foreign policy decisions.

The May 13-15 visit—Trump's first to China in nearly a decade—came amid escalating energy prices following U.S.-Israeli military actions against Iran. Those strikes triggered retaliatory attacks and effectively closed the Strait of Hormuz, a critical passage for approximately 20% of global oil and liquefied natural gas supplies. "The US was the one who requested this meeting, and it's the US economy as a whole and the global economy that is suffering because of [its] aggressive and illegal war on Iran," Haiphong stated.

Despite Trump bringing a delegation of corporate executives including Elon Musk and Boeing's CEO, no major breakthroughs were officially announced before the summit concluded. Haiphong characterized the visit as "about the optics" rather than substantive policy shifts, arguing that genuine improvement in U.S.-China relations requires Washington to abandon aggressive posturing and make significant concessions.

The summit also underscored persistent tensions over Taiwan, with Chinese President Xi Jinping warning Trump that mishandling the self-governed island—claimed by China but armed by the United States—could lead to direct conflict. Both leaders publicly praised the talks as progress toward bilateral stability, though concrete outcomes remained unclear.
