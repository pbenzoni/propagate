In a watershed moment for European security, Germany has published its first-ever comprehensive military strategy, signaling the end of the postwar pacifist consensus that has defined the nation for generations. The document, titled 'Responsibility for Europe,' outlines Berlin's commitment to building what it describes as "the strongest conventional army in Europe" in response to what policymakers view as an existential threat from an expansionist Russia.

The transformation reflects a broader reckoning across Europe about collective security. For decades, Germany's reluctance to project military power was seen as a form of historical accountability. But Russia's aggression has forced a dramatic reassessment. General Carsten Breuer, a key architect of Germany's defense modernization, has characterized potential future conflict with Russia as a "war of necessity"—fundamentally different from discretionary interventions like Afghanistan.

The strategy includes concrete steps that would have been unthinkable just years ago: Germany is deploying its first permanent combat brigade abroad since the 1950s—approximately 4,800 personnel to Lithuania near the Belarusian border as part of NATO's eastern flank. The country is also reconsidering mandatory military service, which was abolished in 2011, with new questionnaires being sent to 18-year-olds about potential service.

While the military buildup is framed entirely as defensive deterrence, the speed and scope of Germany's pivot has raised questions about how Europe can rebuild its defense capabilities without fueling the very confrontation it seeks to prevent. Progressive voices across the continent worry that the current trajectory, while responding to real security threats, may be driven by worst-case assumptions that become self-fulfilling. The challenge facing Germany and its allies is building genuine defensive strength while maintaining diplomatic channels that could prevent escalation into the continental conflict many now fear is inevitable.
