President Trump recently dismissed thousands of casualties across the Middle East as "a little Middle East work" that's going "very well," pledging that Iran would never obtain nuclear weapons. But there's a glaring irony in today's crisis that few in Washington want to acknowledge: Iran's nuclear program began as an American initiative.

Under President Eisenhower's "Atoms for Peace" program in the 1950s, the United States actively exported nuclear technology to allied nations for research, energy, and medicine. The Shah's Iran was a cornerstone partner in this effort. Washington signed agreements with Tehran to develop peaceful nuclear capabilities, viewing the modernization of Iran's energy infrastructure as a strategic investment in a key regional ally. What seemed prudent Cold War strategy then has become today's nuclear nightmare.

The transformation came in 1979 when Islamic revolutionaries overthrew the Shah and seized control of Iran's Western-built nuclear infrastructure. The same facilities and expertise America had helped develop became tools of a radical regime openly hostile to U.S. interests. Now, as Secretary of State Marco Rubio frames the nuclear question as "the reason why we're in this in the first place," he's describing a problem that Washington's own shortsighted policies helped create decades ago.

This history raises uncomfortable questions about interventionist foreign policy and the law of unintended consequences. American taxpayers funded the very nuclear foundation that successive administrations have spent billions trying to dismantle. It's a stark reminder that good intentions in geopolitics can produce disastrous long-term results—and that some partnerships, once established, prove impossible to undo.
