Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth delivered a clear message to America's wealthy European allies at a major security summit in Singapore: the days of relying on U.S. taxpayers to foot the bill for their defense are finished. Speaking at the International Institute for Strategic Studies Shangri-La Dialogue, Hegseth emphasized that America needs "partners, not protectorates" as the Trump administration works to refocus resources on countering China's growing power in the Indo-Pacific.

For years, NATO members have failed to pull their weight. While allies agreed back in 2014 to spend at least 2% of their GDP on defense, many European countries simply ignored that commitment, letting American taxpayers pick up the slack. Even though all 32 NATO members finally hit that modest 2% target in 2025, the United States still shoulders a staggering 60-62% of the alliance's total military spending. Hegseth didn't mince words, calling even the 2% contributions "freeloading" during a question-and-answer session.

The frustration goes beyond dollars and cents. As the Trump administration confronts threats from Iran, some European governments have actively undermined American objectives. Spain has refused to allow U.S. forces to use joint bases for operations against Iran, while France and Germany have pushed for diplomacy instead of decisive action. President Trump himself highlighted this troubling reality, calling it "pretty shocking" that countries claiming to support America's goals "don't want to help."

NATO members agreed last year to work toward spending 5% of GDP on defense by 2035, with a core target of 3.5%. Yet already, European leaders like Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez are balking, calling the goal "unreasonable" and "counterproductive." The pattern is familiar: wealthy European nations enjoying American protection while criticizing American leadership and refusing to invest in their own security. The Trump administration's message is simple—those days are over.
