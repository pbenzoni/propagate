New data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention shows that birth rates in the United States have fallen to historic lows, dropping below levels last seen during the Great Depression. In 2025, there were 53.1 births per 1,000 women of childbearing age, marking a 23% decline since 2007.

The statistics reveal a striking gap between population growth and family formation: while the U.S. population has nearly doubled from 184 million in 1961 to 342 million today, annual births have actually fallen from 4.3 million to 3.6 million over the same period. Public health experts point to multiple structural factors, including rising childcare costs, student debt burdens, lack of paid family leave, and economic insecurity that make starting families increasingly difficult for young Americans.

The sustained decline in fertility rates raises important questions about the support systems available to families who want children. Many economists and demographers argue that countries with stronger social safety nets—including universal healthcare, subsidized childcare, and robust parental leave policies—tend to maintain higher birth rates. The data underscores growing calls for policy reforms that would make family formation more accessible and economically viable for all Americans, regardless of income level.
