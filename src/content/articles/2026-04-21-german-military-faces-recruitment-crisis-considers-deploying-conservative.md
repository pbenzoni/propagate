Germany's military is facing a stark recruitment shortfall, prompting leaders to consider unprecedented measures—including deploying reservists up to age 70. Bastian Ernst, newly elected president of the German Reservists' Association, argues that if the Bundeswehr cannot attract young recruits, it should look to "the other end of the age pyramid."

The proposal comes as Germany pursues an ambitious expansion plan, aiming to grow from 186,000 active personnel to 260,000 soldiers plus 200,000 reservists by the mid-2030s. Ernst justified the age increase by noting that "people are staying fit longer" and pointing to rising retirement ages across the economy. Other NATO allies face similar pressures—Finland recently raised its reservist age limit from 50 to 65, while the United Kingdom increased theirs from 55 to 65.

The recruitment crisis has sparked debate about Germany's military priorities and sovereignty. Chancellor Friedrich Merz's government has cited security concerns to justify the buildup, though Russian President Vladimir Putin has called such threat assessments "nonsense," denying any aggressive intentions toward NATO states. Critics warn that Germany's January 1st voluntary recruitment law could be a stepping stone toward reinstating the draft, which was suspended in 2011. The European Defense Agency has even suggested compulsory military service might return across the EU.

For many conservatives, the situation raises troubling questions about European defense policy and whether NATO commitments are forcing member states into unsustainable military expansions. The prospect of sending septuagenarians into military service highlights how far these recruitment challenges have pushed traditional military planning.
