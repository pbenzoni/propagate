The European Union's latest migration agreement has been heralded by politicians as "historic" and the "strictest-ever" approach to border control. The new rules would allow EU countries to transfer rejected asylum seekers to third countries and introduce tougher measures for dealing with illegal migrants. Yet beneath the rhetoric lies a familiar pattern: lawmakers promise action while carefully preserving the very structures that created the crisis.

The gap between political promises and actual enforcement has become a hallmark of Western governance. While new procedures and databases appear on paper, the underlying economic incentives driving mass migration remain untouched. Politicians deliver reassuring speeches about defending borders, but avoid confronting the businesses and industries that benefit from cheap foreign labor. The result is political theater designed to placate concerned citizens without challenging powerful economic interests.

This contradiction exists on both sides of the Atlantic. A government truly committed to ending illegal immigration would focus on employers, labor contractors, and industries dependent on foreign workers. Yet such measures would provoke opposition from corporations that profit from depressed wages and expanded labor pools. Instead, enforcement remains largely symbolic—high-profile arrests generate dramatic footage while the broader economic machinery continues operating unchanged.

The costs of mass migration get distributed across society through strained infrastructure, overcrowded schools, burdened healthcare systems, and expanded welfare programs. Meanwhile, the benefits remain concentrated among businesses enjoying access to cheaper labor. As one political thinker observed, criticizing immigration while ignoring capitalism—or vice versa—misses the fundamental relationship between the two. Until policymakers address the economic foundations driving migration, new laws will remain little more than empty promises.
