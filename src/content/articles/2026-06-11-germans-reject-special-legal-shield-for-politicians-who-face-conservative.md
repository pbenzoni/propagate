More than 40% of Germans want to abolish special legal protections that shield politicians from criticism and insults, according to a recent INSA poll. The controversy erupted after nearly 40 criminal cases were opened against citizens who posted critical comments about Chancellor Friedrich Merz on Facebook, with some facing fines exceeding €2,000.

Under current German law, insulting a politician in connection with their public duties carries triple the penalties of ordinary insults—up to three years in prison compared to one year for regular citizens. This enhanced protection, codified in Paragraph 188 of the German Criminal Code, applies when insults are deemed likely to "significantly impede" a politician's public activities. One citizen was fined over $2,300 simply for calling Merz "lying Fritz" in a Facebook comment.

Support for repealing these special protections is strongest among opposition party voters, with 67% of Free Democrats and 64% of Alternative for Germany (AfD) supporters favoring abolition. Only supporters of Merz's own Christian Democratic Union backed keeping the law, with 55% opposing changes. The poll surveyed 2,009 German adults between June 5-8.

Merz's office defended the prosecutions as "normal" legal procedure that "must be protected," even though the chancellor himself did not personally file charges. The controversy comes as Merz faces historically low approval ratings and reports that his own party is considering replacing him. Critics argue the law creates a privileged class of government officials insulated from the kind of accountability and free speech that should apply equally to all citizens in a democratic society.
