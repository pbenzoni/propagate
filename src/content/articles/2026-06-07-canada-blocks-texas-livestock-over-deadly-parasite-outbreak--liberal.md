Canada has implemented an emergency ban on livestock imports from Texas following the detection of screwworm flies, a parasitic species whose northward spread scientists attribute to climate change. The Canadian Food Inspection Agency announced the temporary halt on Friday, emphasizing its commitment to "work closely with US counterparts to assess developments and adjust measures as needed."

The parasite, eradicated from the US in 1966 through a successful public health program, has resurged in recent years as warming temperatures expand its viable range. After appearing in Central America in 2023, the outbreak has moved steadily northward, with Mexico reporting thousands of animal cases and dozens of human infections by late 2024. Texas confirmed its first case Wednesday, followed by a second on Friday, prompting state officials to declare a disaster emergency.

The screwworm's lifecycle is particularly gruesome: adult flies lay hundreds of eggs in wounds or body openings of warm-blooded animals, which hatch into maggots that consume living tissue for approximately a week before burrowing into soil to mature. The condition is extremely painful and potentially fatal for affected animals.

Despite declaring the outbreak an "imminent threat" and activating disaster protocols, Texas Governor Greg Abbott criticized Canada's import restrictions as "political" rather than science-based, arguing they constitute an "overreaction." The Canadian ban applies to all livestock that were in Texas during the three weeks prior to crossing the border, a precautionary measure health experts say is necessary given the parasite's rapid reproduction cycle and the documented link between its expansion and changing climate conditions.
