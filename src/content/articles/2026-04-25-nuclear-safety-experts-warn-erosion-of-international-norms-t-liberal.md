Four decades after the Chernobyl disaster transformed nuclear safety consciousness, international experts are sounding alarms about the dangerous erosion of protections for civilian nuclear infrastructure. The psychological and diplomatic barriers that once made nuclear facilities virtually untouchable in warfare are showing troubling signs of collapse.

Historically, nuclear power plants have been largely protected by an informal taboo—borrowing from the global consensus against nuclear weapons themselves. Past conflicts saw limited targeting of facilities still under construction, with attacks on Iraq's Osiraq reactor in the 1980s and Syria's suspected reactor in 2007. These preemptive strikes, while controversial, avoided the catastrophic scenario of attacking operational facilities with active reactors and stored radioactive material.

Today's conflicts reveal a more disturbing pattern. Recent years have witnessed strikes near operational nuclear facilities, including Iran's Bushehr plant and contested sites in Ukraine. International Atomic Energy Agency observers have documented attacks in the vicinity of active nuclear infrastructure—a dangerous escalation that nuclear safety advocates warn could lead to environmental catastrophe affecting millions of civilians far beyond any conflict zone.

The weakening of these norms represents not just a regional security crisis, but a global threat. Radiation recognizes no borders, and the international community's failure to enforce strict protections for nuclear facilities could set precedents that endanger communities worldwide. As geopolitical tensions rise, experts emphasize that strengthening—not abandoning—the multilateral frameworks designed to protect civilian infrastructure must be an urgent priority.
