A groundbreaking analysis of a 250-million-year-old fossil has confirmed that early mammal ancestors laid eggs, offering new insights into how life adapted during one of Earth's most devastating climate catastrophes. The research, published in the journal PLOS One, represents a major scientific breakthrough made possible by advanced imaging technology.

Professor Jennifer Botha, a South African scientist leading the research, explained that the fossil—a perfectly curled Lystrosaurus hatchling—was discovered in 2008 but required years of technological advancement before it could be properly studied. Using synchrotron X-ray CT scanning, which creates ultra-high-resolution 3D images without damaging delicate specimens, researchers confirmed the hatchling had died within its egg.

The Lystrosaurus, a herbivorous mammal ancestor, survived and thrived after the End-Permian Mass Extinction approximately 252 million years ago—a climate catastrophe believed to have been triggered by massive volcanic eruptions and coal burning. The resulting rapid global warming created extreme heat and environmental instability, wiping out up to 96% of marine species and 70% of land vertebrates.

Researchers determined that Lystrosaurus eggs were soft and leathery rather than hard-shelled, making fossil preservation extremely rare. The species likely laid large eggs adapted to resist drying out in the hot, arid post-extinction environment. Their young apparently hatched at an advanced developmental stage, ready to feed themselves and survive in the harsh conditions—an evolutionary adaptation that proved crucial for mammalian ancestors' survival during Earth's worst extinction event.
