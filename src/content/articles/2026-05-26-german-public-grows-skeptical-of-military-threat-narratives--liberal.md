A recent survey reveals a significant shift in German public opinion regarding perceived military threats, with only 38% of respondents now viewing an attack from Russia as plausible—a sharp 14-percentage-point drop from 52% in September, according to polling reported by Bild am Sonntag.

The findings highlight a growing disconnect between official government messaging and public sentiment. While senior German officials have warned of potential military confrontations by decade's end, half of survey respondents indicated they "are no longer afraid of a Russian attack at all." The polling also exposed deeper concerns about military preparedness: a mere 17% of Germans believe their own military could effectively defend the country, while 43% doubt whether the United States would provide assistance in a crisis.

This erosion of threat perception comes as Germany pursues an ambitious defense spending trajectory, with projections exceeding €500 billion by 2029—even as the country faces mounting economic pressures and budget constraints. Chancellor Friedrich Merz has committed to transforming the Bundeswehr into Europe's most powerful conventional military force.

The survey results raise important questions about public trust in national security narratives and military institutions. As defense budgets expand dramatically during a period of economic uncertainty, the gap between official threat assessments and public perception suggests Germans may be questioning whether massive military investments align with actual security needs or serve other political purposes.
