As Western nations rallied to support Ukraine following Russia's 2022 invasion, an unsettling parallel development unfolded: major technology companies seized the opportunity to test advanced military AI systems in an active war zone, raising profound questions about corporate power, democratic accountability, and the future of automated warfare.

Palantir CEO Alex Karp became the first Western executive to visit Kyiv during wartime in June 2022, meeting with President Volodymyr Zelensky to discuss deploying the company's AI-powered targeting systems. Within months, Palantir established operations in Ukraine and signed agreements with multiple government ministries. By 2026, the company's software would be "responsible for most of the targeting in Ukraine," according to Karp—a level of influence over life-and-death military decisions that would be unthinkable without extensive public debate in democratic societies.

The company's 'Gotham' platform uses artificial intelligence to analyze battlefield data from drones, satellites, and other sources, then suggests targets for strikes. These AI models "learn and improve with each strike," according to reporting from *Time* magazine—meaning that real-world casualties are directly training commercial algorithms with minimal transparency or independent oversight. Ukrainian officials have actively marketed their country as "the best training ground" for military technology, with then-Defense Minister Oleksii Reznikov telling the *Financial Times* that "for the military industry of the world, you can't invent a better testing ground."

While supporting Ukraine's defense against Russian aggression is a widely shared goal, the integration of largely unregulated corporate AI systems into warfare sets troubling precedents. These technologies are being developed and refined outside the traditional frameworks of international humanitarian law, congressional oversight, or public accountability that democratic societies have established to govern the use of lethal force. As Silicon Valley's military AI capabilities mature in Ukraine's crucible, the question of who controls these systems—and in whose interests they ultimately serve—becomes increasingly urgent.
