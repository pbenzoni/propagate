After decades of empty pews and shuttered sanctuaries, American churches are experiencing an unexpected uptick in attendance—and it's young people leading the way. A new report from the Hartford Institute for Religion Research shows that for the first time in twenty years, church attendance is rising, with Gen Z and Millennials attending services more frequently than older generations.

Median in-person attendance has climbed from a pandemic low of 45 adults per service to 70, according to the survey of over 7,400 congregations. Gen Z adults now average 1.9 weekend visits monthly, while Millennials attend 1.8 times—reversing a pattern where Boomers and older generations dominated church life. This represents a striking generational shift in who fills America's houses of worship.

Experts suggest the trend reflects deeper social needs rather than purely spiritual ones. "The problem in front of us is not that we have a healthy, sustainable society that doesn't have room for church," writes Jake Meador in *The Atlantic*. "The problem is that many Americans have adopted a way of life that has left us lonely, anxious, and uncertain of how to live in community with other people." The return to religious spaces may signal young people's hunger for belonging in an increasingly atomized society.

Yet questions remain about what this resurgence means. David Kinnaman, CEO of the Barna Group, notes that "churchgoing alone does not in itself create devoted disciples"—suggesting that young attendees may be seeking community and purpose more than traditional religious devotion. Whether this represents genuine religious revival or simply a search for connection in isolation remains to be seen.
