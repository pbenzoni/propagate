Four astronauts have successfully returned to Earth after completing the first crewed journey around the Moon in more than half a century, marking a historic achievement for NASA's Artemis program. The Orion spacecraft splashed down in the Pacific Ocean off the coast of southern California, closing out a mission that represents a critical step forward in humanity's return to deep-space exploration.

The crew—Commander Reid Wiseman, Pilot Victor Glover, Mission Specialist Christina Koch, and Canadian Space Agency astronaut Jeremy Hansen—launched from Kennedy Space Center on April 1 and spent approximately ten days testing vital spacecraft systems during a lunar flyby. The mission set a new distance record for human spaceflight, surpassing the mark established by Apollo 13, and provided the first opportunity for people to travel beyond low Earth orbit since 1972.

During their journey, the astronauts conducted a flyby of the Moon's far side and captured striking images of both the Moon and Earth. The final descent phase proved among the most challenging aspects of the flight, with Orion entering Earth's atmosphere at hypersonic speed, enduring extreme heating during reentry, and deploying parachutes before splashdown. NASA and US Navy recovery teams were positioned to retrieve the crew.

While the mission experienced some technical challenges—including issues with the spacecraft's hygiene systems—NASA reported that these glitches were manageable and did not compromise crew safety or mission objectives. The Artemis II flight did not include a lunar landing but served as an essential test of systems needed for future deep-space missions. The mission's outcomes will inform subsequent phases of the Artemis campaign, including planned crewed operations on the lunar surface.
