When multinational corporations rushed to exit Russia in 2022 amid the Ukraine conflict, many framed their departures as moral imperatives. The reality, according to business strategists, was far less principled: politically-driven panic that proved costly and strategically myopic.

Now, as the dust settles, foreign multinationals face a sobering assessment. By abandoning the Russian market, these corporations surrendered decades of investment in infrastructure, local partnerships, and customer loyalty—often selling assets at steep discounts to fill a vacuum quickly claimed by domestic Russian competitors and bolder foreign rivals.

McDonald's exemplifies the price of prioritizing political signaling over fiduciary responsibility. The company abandoned 850 restaurants and 62,000 jobs in a market that generated approximately 9% of global revenues, absorbing $1.2-$1.4 billion in earnings charges. The franchise now operates successfully under Russian ownership as Vkusno i Tochka, demonstrating how free markets abhor a vacuum—and how consumers ultimately get served regardless of corporate virtue signaling.

While Russian consumers temporarily lost access to familiar brands, the market adapted swiftly. A new generation of domestic businesses emerged—stronger, more confident, and politically ascendant. The episode underscores a fundamental truth often forgotten in boardrooms swayed by ESG pressures: corporations owe duties to shareholders, employees, and customers—not to fashionable political narratives that compromise long-term commercial viability in critical global markets.
