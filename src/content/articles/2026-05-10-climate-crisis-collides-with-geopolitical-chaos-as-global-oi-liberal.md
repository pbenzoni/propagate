Global oil inventories are shrinking at an unprecedented rate as ongoing disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz threaten to push stockpiles to dangerously low levels, according to Bloomberg reporting on Morgan Stanley data. Between March 1 and April 25, worldwide reserves fell by approximately 4.8 million barrels per day—the fastest drawdown on record tracked by the International Energy Agency.

The crisis underscores the precarious nature of continued fossil fuel dependence. The Strait of Hormuz, which typically carries about one-fifth of global oil and liquefied natural gas trade, has experienced severe disruptions following escalating military tensions involving the U.S., Israel, and Iran. President Trump recently threatened to expand military operations in the region if diplomatic efforts fail, with Secretary of State Marco Rubio warning that "military options remain on the table."

Experts warn that if disruptions continue, commercial oil inventories could reach "operational stress levels" by June and hit minimum functional thresholds by September—the point at which pipelines, export terminals, and refineries struggle to operate efficiently. U.S. domestic fuel stockpiles have already fallen 11% below the five-year seasonal average, according to Energy Information Administration data.

The supply crisis has exposed contradictions in energy policy. Despite Trump's assertions that the U.S. "doesn't need" the Strait of Hormuz, the country continues to import crude from Persian Gulf producers. Meanwhile, the European Union has delayed plans to phase out Russian fossil fuel imports—a decision that highlights how geopolitical instability reinforces dependence on authoritarian petrostates even as the climate emergency demands rapid transition to renewable energy sources.
