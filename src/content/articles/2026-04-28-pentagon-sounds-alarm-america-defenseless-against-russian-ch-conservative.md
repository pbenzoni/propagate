In stark testimony before the Senate Armed Services Committee, Pentagon officials delivered a sobering assessment: the United States currently possesses "no defense against hypersonic weapons or cruise missiles" being deployed by adversaries Russia and China.

The admission came as military leaders sought funding for President Trump's Golden Dome initiative—a comprehensive missile defense modernization program that would reverse decades of strategic vulnerability. Assistant Secretary of War for Space Policy Marc Berkowitz told lawmakers that America's existing homeland defense system was "specifically designed against a small scale rogue attack" and is woefully inadequate against peer competitors.

Space Force's Michael Guetlein, who leads the Golden Dome program, detailed how both Russia and China have aggressively modernized their arsenals with hypersonic glide vehicles and advanced cruise missiles like Russia's nuclear-powered Burevestnik—weapons "designed to challenge the tracking and engagement capabilities of our sensors." These systems represent a direct threat to American sovereignty that previous administrations allowed to develop unchecked.

The vulnerability traces back to the Bush administration's 2002 withdrawal from the Cold War-era Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty—a move Moscow claims forced their investment in weapons designed to circumvent American defenses. Further complicating matters, recent conflicts have reportedly depleted U.S. interceptor stockpiles for systems like THAAD and Patriot, with replenishment potentially requiring years.

President Trump's Golden Dome system aims to restore American strategic superiority through expanded ground- and space-based capabilities. While initial cost estimates hover around $175-185 billion over the next decade, military experts emphasize that national security cannot be measured in dollars alone. The question facing lawmakers is straightforward: Can America afford to remain defenseless against adversaries who have spent years preparing to defeat us?
