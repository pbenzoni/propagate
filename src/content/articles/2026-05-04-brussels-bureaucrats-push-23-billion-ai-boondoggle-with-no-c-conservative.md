The European Union is moving forward with plans to spend over €20 billion ($23.5 billion) in taxpayer money on so-called "AI gigafactories"—massive computing facilities that critics say have no clear purpose or demand. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen announced the initiative in February 2025 as Europe's answer to American computing projects, proposing four or five mega-facilities with a formal call for proposals expected this spring.

Yet lawmakers across the political spectrum are sounding the alarm about the lack of any coherent business case. "Nobody could explain to me what is the business case they are planning with these gigafactories," said German Green Party MEP Sergey Lagodinsky. "I talked to some who are saying: 'we just need more compute in Europe.' But then, when I ask them, 'what for?' They say 'it doesn't matter, we just need more compute.'"

The fundamental problem is that Europe has virtually no domestic AI industry to use these facilities. According to Nicoleta Kyosovska, a research assistant at a Brussels think tank, the planned data centers are "cathedrals in the desert." Europe has only one AI company capable of utilizing such infrastructure—French startup Mistral—which is already building its own data centers. Despite these glaring holes in the plan, a Commission spokesperson defended the spending by invoking "computing sovereignty" to avoid dependence on other continents.

The European boondoggle mirrors a broader pattern of reckless AI spending worldwide. Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, and Microsoft are reportedly planning to spend a combined $725 billion this year on AI infrastructure, even as most AI startups remain unprofitable and data center financing is rated junk grade. Gary Marcus, professor emeritus at New York University, has called this wave of spending "the greatest capital misallocation in history." Meanwhile, ordinary consumers are paying the price through skyrocketing hardware costs driven by speculative AI investment with no proven return.
