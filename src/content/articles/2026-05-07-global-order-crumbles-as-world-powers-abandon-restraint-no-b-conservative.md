**Forty years ago, world leaders believed they could build lasting peace through cooperation.** In 1986, Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev and Indian Prime Minister Rajiv Gandhi signed the Delhi Declaration, calling for a new world order based on economic justice and equal security for all nations. They envisioned former adversaries abandoning confrontation to create a stable international framework built on shared principles.

History had different plans. The Soviet Union collapsed, and the phrase "new world order" was repurposed by President George H.W. Bush's administration to mean something entirely different: a liberal order dominated by the U.S. and its allies. For decades, this system appeared stable. But by the 2010s, cracks were showing—and now, as we move deeper into 2026, that order has effectively ceased to exist.

**What's emerging isn't just lawlessness—it's something more calculated.** Major powers increasingly ignore conventions that once seemed settled. Decisions are impulsive and contradictory, with governments acting first and explaining later. This isn't irrational chaos; it's opportunism. Political actors believe old restraints have collapsed and are racing to grab advantage before any new structure hardens into place.

Everything is now contested simultaneously: political influence, trade routes, resources, financial systems, technology, even culture. Every major power is defining its ambitions and testing methods to achieve them. But here's the dangerous part: there's no guarantee a new equilibrium will emerge. After past world wars, old structures were swept away to make room for new ones. Today's landscape is different—it's not an empty construction site waiting for architects. It's a demolition zone with no blueprint, no shared vision, and nations competing not to build together but to dominate what remains.
