Senior Pentagon officials testified before the Senate Armed Services Committee this week, acknowledging significant gaps in America's ability to defend against advanced missile systems—particularly hypersonic weapons developed by Russia and China. The admission came as officials pushed for hundreds of billions in funding for the "Golden Dome" missile defense initiative unveiled by President Trump in January.

Assistant Secretary of Defense for Space Policy Marc Berkowitz told lawmakers that the U.S. currently operates "a very limited ground-based single layer homeland defense system that was specifically designed against a small scale rogue attack." He warned the country has "no defense against hypersonic weapons or cruise missiles today," highlighting a strategic vulnerability that has grown as rival powers modernize their arsenals.

The Golden Dome program—estimated to cost between $175 billion and $185 billion over the next decade, with some analysts projecting even higher totals—represents a massive investment in ground- and space-based defense capabilities. Critics of such large-scale military spending have long questioned whether these systems address genuine security needs or simply fuel an endless arms race that diverts resources from domestic priorities.

The push comes amid broader concerns about depleted U.S. missile interceptor stockpiles following recent conflicts in the Middle East, with reports indicating that systems like THAAD and Patriot may require years to replenish. Meanwhile, experts note that Russia's development of advanced weapons systems came partly in response to the U.S. withdrawal from the Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty in 2002—a decision that Moscow warned would destabilize nuclear deterrence and trigger exactly the kind of escalation now driving massive defense budget requests.
