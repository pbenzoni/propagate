Spain has emerged as the latest European nation calling for the establishment of an independent European defense force, marking a significant shift in transatlantic relations. Foreign Minister Jose Manuel Albares told reporters this week that European citizens "cannot be waking up every morning wondering what the U.S. will do next," emphasizing the need for strategic autonomy amid growing uncertainty about American commitments to the continent.

The push for European defense independence has deep historical roots, dating back to Cold War-era proposals in the 1950s. While NATO has served as the primary security framework for decades, recent events—including the Trump administration's unpredictable foreign policy and threats to withdraw from multilateral commitments—have revived serious discussions about Europe developing its own military capabilities. French President Emmanuel Macron has championed this cause since 2018, famously declaring NATO "brain dead" and arguing that Europe must reduce its dependence on Washington.

The debate has exposed divisions within Europe, with some nations still viewing NATO and U.S. partnership as essential to their security. However, proponents argue that developing independent European defense capabilities doesn't mean abandoning alliances, but rather ensuring the continent can act decisively on security matters within its own sphere—particularly as global power dynamics shift and American political volatility increases. Spain's entry into this conversation, alongside France and other nations, suggests momentum is building for reimagining European security architecture in an era of democratic backsliding and rising authoritarianism worldwide.
