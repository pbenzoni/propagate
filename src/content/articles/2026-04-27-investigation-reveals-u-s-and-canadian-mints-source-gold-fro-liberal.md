A New York Times investigation has uncovered that U.S. and Canadian government mints have been sourcing gold from mines controlled by Colombia's Clan del Golfo drug cartel, despite federal regulations meant to ensure domestic supply chains. The revelations raise serious questions about regulatory oversight and environmental standards in precious metal procurement.

According to the investigation, a Texas-based refiner, Dillon Gage—one of the main suppliers to the U.S. Mint—has been mixing Colombian gold with American supplies, effectively laundering the foreign metal into the domestic supply chain. Workers in northwestern Colombia extract this gold using mercury, a highly toxic substance that poses severe health risks to laborers and causes lasting environmental damage. Some mining operations were even observed on Colombian military base premises, highlighting the brazen nature of the illegal activity.

The U.S. Mint initially insisted all its gold came from domestic sources as required by federal law, claiming production connects "to the founding principles of our nation." However, when confronted with the Times' findings, officials backpedaled, describing the U.S. as merely the "primary" source. Critically, the Mint conducts no verification of gold origins from its contractors.

The Times noted that even violations of Canadian guidelines carry no legal consequences. The findings expose significant gaps in supply chain transparency and environmental protections, undermining both nations' stated commitments to ethical sourcing and worker safety standards.
