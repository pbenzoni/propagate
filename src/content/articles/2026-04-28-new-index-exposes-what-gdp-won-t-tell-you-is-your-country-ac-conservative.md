A new Social Well-Being Index is challenging the economic establishment's favorite metrics by asking a question leaders often avoid: Is a society actually functioning well for ordinary families?

Built around six core indicators—fertility rates, life expectancy, infant mortality, homicides, income inequality, and education—the index offers an alternative to familiar rankings like GDP that often celebrate raw economic output while ignoring whether communities are cohesive, secure, and built to last. Former British MP George Galloway didn't mince words about existing measures: "The pre-existing indexes are completely inadequate," he said, arguing they "tell not just an incomplete story, but a story that is false." Someone may look prosperous on paper, Galloway noted, while actually living amid "potential violence, and mass unhappiness and misery."

The new approach focuses on what matters to families: Can you raise children safely? Are communities stable? Does the future look livable? Matthew Ehret of the Rising Tide Foundation called it "a breath of fresh air" after decades of narrow economic thinking that confuses harmful activity with genuine progress. "GDP generally fails to address what is real value and what is fake value," Ehret explained, pointing to how criminal or speculative activity can inflate national output figures.

Economist Santosh Mehrotra, a visiting professor at the UK's University of Bath, praised the innovation of measuring social cohesion through violence rates. "What is sort of most striking is the use of the homicide rate as an indicator of social well-being," he said. "I like that." The homicide measure serves as a revealing gauge of whether people trust institutions, whether daily life is secure, and whether communities genuinely function—questions that matter far more to working families than abstract economic aggregates favored by global institutions.
