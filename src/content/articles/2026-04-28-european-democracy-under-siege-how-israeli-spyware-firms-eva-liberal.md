A troubling pattern of political interference by Israeli intelligence-linked private firms has emerged across Europe, yet the European Union's leadership has remained conspicuously silent—raising urgent questions about accountability and the protection of democratic norms.

Two recent cases involving Black Cube, a private intelligence company staffed by former Mossad and Israeli Defense Forces agents, illustrate the threat. In Cyprus, operatives posing as investors secretly recorded conversations with associates of President Nikos Christodoulides, releasing videos that triggered resignations and undermined the country's EU presidency. Months later in Slovenia, similar tactics targeted associates of liberal Prime Minister Robert Golob just days before parliamentary elections, with recordings suspected to have been commissioned by his right-wing predecessor.

Despite Slovenia's government explicitly labeling these activities "a clear hybrid threat against the European Union" and formally requesting an investigation, the European Commission has not publicly acknowledged the complaint. This silence is particularly striking given the EU's vocal concerns about election interference from other sources—and the commission's willingness to deploy censorship tools in response to unproven allegations elsewhere.

The problem extends beyond Black Cube. Pegasus spyware, developed by Israel's NSO Group, has been deployed in nearly a dozen EU countries since 2016 to surveil journalists, political opponents, and even EU leaders themselves. The European Parliament has called for "more rigorous control mechanisms" on such technology, yet meaningful action remains elusive. As surveillance tools increasingly threaten press freedom and democratic governance, civil liberties advocates warn that the EU's failure to respond effectively undermines its stated commitment to human rights and the rule of law.
