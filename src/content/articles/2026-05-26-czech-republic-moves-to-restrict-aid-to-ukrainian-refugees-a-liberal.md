The Czech government has approved legislation that would significantly curtail benefits and residency rights for Ukrainian refugees, reflecting a troubling trend across Europe as countries retreat from their initial commitments to those fleeing Russia's invasion.

The proposed measures, which still require parliamentary approval, would mandate that Ukrainians receiving humanitarian aid either work, run a business, or actively seek employment. Recipients would also be required to spend at least 16 days per month in the Czech Republic to qualify for payments, with exemptions only for children, students, and pensioners. The legislation would strip temporary protection status from those who spend more than 30 days outside the Schengen Area or face criminal or administrative penalties.

Interior Minister Lubomir Metnar cited concerns that Ukrainian refugees enjoyed "advantages over local residents," a framing that advocates say scapegoats vulnerable populations for broader systemic challenges. The Czech Republic hosts approximately 385,000 Ukrainian migrants among its 10 million residents, one of the highest per capita refugee populations in the EU. Prime Minister Andrej Babis characterized the crackdown as part of a "security push," language that critics argue unfairly criminalizes those seeking safety from war.

The rollback comes as more than 4.35 million Ukrainians are registered for temporary protection across the EU, according to Eurostat. Poland, Germany, and Hungary have already moved to cut benefits, signaling a concerning erosion of solidarity with refugees across the continent. Human rights advocates warn that such restrictions not only violate humanitarian principles but also reflect growing anti-immigrant sentiment being exploited for political gain.
