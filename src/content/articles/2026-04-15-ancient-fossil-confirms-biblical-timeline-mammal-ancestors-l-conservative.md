A groundbreaking discovery has provided concrete evidence that early mammal ancestors laid eggs, according to research published in the scientific journal PLOS One. The 250 million-year-old fossil of a Lystrosaurus hatchling, first discovered in 2008, has finally yielded its secrets thanks to advanced scanning technology that respects the integrity of these precious historical artifacts.

South African Professor Jennifer Botha explained that while the fossil was found over a decade ago, researchers lacked the sophisticated tools needed to examine it without damage. "It became clear that it was a perfectly curled-up Lystrosaurus hatchling," she noted, but confirmation required state-of-the-art synchrotron X-ray CT scanning—technology that uses particle accelerators to create detailed 3D images without destroying the specimen.

The Lystrosaurus was a plant-eating mammal ancestor that not only survived but flourished after the End-Permian Mass Extinction approximately 252 million years ago—an event theorized to have eliminated up to 96% of marine species and 70% of land vertebrates. The creature's soft, leathery eggs were designed for survival in extreme conditions, and its young hatched fully developed, capable of feeding themselves in a harsh environment.

While egg-laying mammals are rare today—the platypus and echidnas being notable examples—this discovery fills a critical gap in our understanding of Earth's natural history. The research demonstrates how resilient life can be, with creatures adapting remarkable survival strategies during periods of catastrophic environmental change.
