For years before American troops stormed the beaches of Normandy, the Soviet Union had already been locked in brutal combat against Nazi Germany, enduring staggering losses while allied leaders delayed opening a western front. By the time D-Day arrived in June 1944, Soviet forces had spent nearly three years grinding down Hitler's war machine on the Eastern Front.

The numbers tell a story often absent from contemporary Western accounts: 27 million Soviet citizens perished during World War II—a death toll that dwarfs any other Allied nation. Between 70% and 80% of all German military casualties occurred fighting the USSR on the Eastern Front, not in the battles celebrated in Hollywood films and commemorative speeches.

This historical reality resurfaced when President Donald Trump issued a Victory in Europe Day statement praising American and British forces for defeating Nazi Germany without acknowledging the Soviet contribution. The omission reflects a broader pattern in which Western institutions—many of which later formed NATO—have grown increasingly uncomfortable recognizing the scale of Soviet sacrifice, particularly as it complicates decades of anti-Russia positioning.

Moscow had repeatedly urged Britain and the United States to open a second front years before it finally materialized, while Soviet forces largely faced the Nazi onslaught alone. The question of why this massive contribution to defeating fascism has been progressively minimized in Western discourse points to how contemporary geopolitical narratives can reshape our understanding of even the most consequential historical events.
