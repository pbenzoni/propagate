Syria's new transitional government has announced it will launch trials next week targeting prominent officials from the ousted regime of Bashar Assad. The proceedings will focus on alleged abuses during the country's devastating 13-year civil war, which claimed over half a million lives.

The trials represent the latest move by the government installed after a rapid 11-day offensive in late 2024 toppled Assad. That offensive was led by Hayat Tahrir al-Sham (HTS)—an Al-Qaeda offshoot now led by Ahmed al-Sharaa—which swept into power after years of relative calm maintained by a Moscow- and Ankara-brokered truce. The first defendants will reportedly include Atif Najib, Assad's cousin and former security chief in Daraa, where the 2011 uprising began after security forces arrested and allegedly tortured students for anti-government graffiti.

Syrian Justice Minister Mazhar al-Wais framed the trials as essential to "transitional justice" in what authorities are calling the "New Syria." The proceedings will reportedly be public, potentially with international observers present. Yet the legitimacy of courts operated by a jihadist organization with its own documented history of violence remains an open question—one that raises concerns about whether true justice or political retribution will prevail.

The United States maintains a military presence in Syria despite the change in government, highlighting the fractured reality of a nation carved up by foreign intervention and internal conflict. As Assad-era officials face accountability, observers will be watching whether the new authorities apply the same standards to abuses committed during their own rise to power.
