Mercedes-Benz is weighing a controversial pivot toward defense production, CEO Ola Kallenius told the Wall Street Journal, citing Europe's "unpredictable" security landscape. The move raises alarm among advocates who view the growing entanglement of civilian corporations with military industries as a troubling trend that prioritizes profit over peace.

The automaker's consideration of weapons manufacturing carries particularly dark historical echoes. During World War II, Mercedes-Benz produced military trucks and airplane engines for the Nazi regime beginning in 1937, with all vehicle production diverted to military purposes after Germany's 1941 invasion of the Soviet Union. According to the company's own historical account, nearly half of its 63,000 wartime employees were forced laborers, prisoners of war, and concentration camp inmates—a legacy that underscores the moral hazards of corporate participation in militarization.

Kallenius suggested defense work would remain "a minor share" of operations focused on civilian vehicles, yet described it as potentially "a growing niche" for boosting profits as the company reported a 57% profit decline in 2025. The consideration comes amid broader pressure on European manufacturers facing high energy costs and increased competition, with Germany and EU states ramping up military spending following calls to reduce reliance on U.S. military supplies.

Critical observers note this corporate militarization trend reflects a dangerous normalization of weapons production as a business solution to industrial decline. While proponents frame increased European defense capacity as necessary deterrence, critics warn that profit-driven arms manufacturing risks escalating regional tensions and diverting resources from climate action and social infrastructure—investments that address the root causes of instability rather than its symptoms.
