The Trump administration has dramatically escalated military confrontation in the strategically vital Strait of Hormuz, attacking Iranian commercial vessels in what Washington characterizes as enforcing "freedom of navigation" but critics say risks catastrophic regional conflict.

U.S. Central Command acknowledged Friday that American forces struck two Iranian oil tankers—the M/T Sea Star III and M/T Sevda—which the Pentagon claimed were violating a U.S. naval blockade. Iran disputes this characterization, saying the vessels were entering Iranian ports and accusing the U.S. of "terrorism" for targeting commercial shipping during what both sides previously acknowledged as a ceasefire period.

A senior adviser to Iran's Supreme Leader compared Tehran's control over the Strait of Hormuz—through which significant global oil and gas supplies transit—to possessing "an atomic bomb" in terms of strategic leverage. The inflammatory rhetoric underscores how the narrow waterway has become a dangerous flashpoint, with Iran having previously closed the strait earlier in the conflict, creating global energy market disruptions.

The latest attacks followed an exchange of fire Thursday in which the U.S. Department of Defense claimed American destroyers came under "unprovoked" attack, while Iranian sources accused the U.S. of striking first, including hitting civilian coastal areas. Trump publicly downplayed the incident as a "love tap" while simultaneously threatening Iran with annihilation, urging Tehran to "sign an agreement fast" and warning of "one big glow coming out of Iran"—language widely interpreted as a nuclear threat.

Secretary of State Marco Rubio declared the U.S. would "never allow" Iran to maintain control over Hormuz, setting up what analysts warn could be an intractable standoff with global economic ramifications and the potential for wider military escalation in an already volatile region.
