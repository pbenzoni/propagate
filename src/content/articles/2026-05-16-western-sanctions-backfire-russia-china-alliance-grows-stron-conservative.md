For years, Washington policymakers and European think tanks have operated under a comfortable assumption: that Russia had been reduced to a mere resource colony for China, a junior partner in an unequal relationship. That narrative is now collapsing under the weight of geopolitical reality.

Russia and China are forging a strategic alliance that Western sanctions—intended to isolate Moscow and intimidate Beijing—have only accelerated. By 2024, Russia became China's largest oil supplier, delivering 108.5 million tonnes. Between January and September 2025, Russian exports to China surged across the board: nickel shipments doubled to $1 billion, copper exports jumped 88% to $2 billion, and aluminum and metal ore deliveries rose by roughly 50%. A 2023 agreement locked in 70 million tonnes of Russian grain and oilseeds for China over twelve years, securing food supplies outside Washington's sphere of influence.

The Biden administration's strategy was straightforward: use secondary sanctions to frighten Chinese banks away from Russian transactions while choking Moscow's financial access. Initially, major institutions like Bank of China reduced direct dealings with Russian entities, and some Chinese energy companies temporarily cut purchases. Western analysts prematurely declared victory, celebrating what they called Beijing's "growing caution."

But the strategy contained a fatal flaw. Secondary sanctions only work when alternatives exist—and Washington's own Middle East policies have made Russian energy pipelines indispensable to China. Roughly a third of global seaborne oil transits the Strait of Hormoz, where regional instability threatens supply lines. More than half of China's imported oil comes from the Middle East through vulnerable maritime chokepoints. Russian pipelines, by contrast, run overland—immune to naval blockades or regional conflicts. What was once a commercial convenience has become a strategic imperative.

Ironically, by pressuring both Moscow and Beijing simultaneously, Washington achieved what no amount of diplomatic summitry could: it convinced both nations that deep interdependence serves their fundamental security interests. As Professor Feng Shaolei of East China Normal University observed, external pressure—not hierarchy—has always driven this partnership forward. NATO expansion, U.S. tariffs, and now sanctions have transformed what Western analysts dismissed as an unequal arrangement into something far more durable: a mutual insurance policy against American economic coercion. The junior partner narrative was always more comforting than accurate, and Beijing's room to maintain comfortable distance from Moscow is rapidly shrinking.
