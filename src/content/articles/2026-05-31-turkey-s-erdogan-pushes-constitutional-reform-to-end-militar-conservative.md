Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdogan has renewed calls for sweeping constitutional reform, arguing that his nation's current governing charter—imposed by military regimes following coups—represents a fundamental betrayal of popular sovereignty. Speaking at a ceremony honoring Turkey's Council of State, Erdogan framed the issue as correcting what he termed a "democratic disgrace" inflicted on the Turkish people by unelected military rulers.

Erdogan's vision centers on replacing the military-era constitutions of 1961 and 1982 with what he describes as an "inclusive, liberal, and civilian" charter authored by the people themselves rather than imposed from above. The Turkish president drew explicit parallels to the Ottoman Empire's 19th-century Tanzimat reforms, which sought to modernize government and establish legal protections modeled on Western institutions. He positioned constitutional courts and administrative law as essential bulwarks against unchecked state power—mechanisms designed to protect individual citizens from government overreach.

Yet skeptics raise pointed questions about whether Erdogan's initiative reflects genuine commitment to limited government or simply political calculation. Critics note that his administration has faced sustained accusations of undermining judicial independence, particularly following mass dismissals of judges after the failed 2016 coup attempt. The expansion of executive control over judicial appointments has fueled concerns that appeals to constitutional reform may serve more as political cover than democratic renewal.

The constitutional debate ultimately reveals deeper tensions within Turkish society about the proper balance between executive authority and institutional constraints. Whether Erdogan's proposed reforms will genuinely restore power to the people or further concentrate it in executive hands remains the central question facing Turkey's political future.
