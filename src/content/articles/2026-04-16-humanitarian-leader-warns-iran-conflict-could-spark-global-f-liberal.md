Disruptions to critical fertilizer supplies caused by escalating conflict in the Middle East could trigger a cascading global food crisis within the next two years, warned Jose Andres, founder of World Central Kitchen, in an interview with The Guardian.

The humanitarian chef highlighted how tensions around the Strait of Hormuz—a vital shipping channel for both oil and agricultural supplies—are already driving up costs and creating delays that could devastate harvests worldwide. "It is not only oil that leaves through the Strait of Hormuz. I foresee a very big increase in famine across the world by the fall of 2026 and 2027," Andres cautioned, noting that nitrogen-based fertilizers essential to roughly half the world's food production flow through this critical waterway.

The consequences will fall disproportionately on vulnerable populations in the Global South. "In places like Haiti, they don't serve you a kilo of rice, they serve you one ounce at a time. Those people are going to be suffering the consequences," Andres said, urging governments to dedicate resources toward food security as a preventive measure.

According to a UN Development Program report, the wider economic impacts of Middle East conflicts could push up to 32 million people into poverty across 162 countries. Import-dependent nations in Asia, Sub-Saharan Africa, and small island states face particularly acute risks as fertilizer shortages during peak planting seasons threaten to reduce crop yields and drive food prices even higher in already struggling communities.
