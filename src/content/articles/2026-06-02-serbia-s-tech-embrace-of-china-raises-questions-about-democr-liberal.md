Serbian President Aleksandar Vučić recently completed what he called "the most important trip of his political career" to Beijing, where he received China's Order of Friendship and signed dozens of agreements focusing on AI, robotics, and advanced manufacturing.

The partnership represents a significant shift from earlier Chinese investments in Serbian infrastructure and heavy industry. Now, nearly €1 billion in new commitments target cutting-edge sectors including humanoid robotics, artificial intelligence, and digital infrastructure. Serbia is positioning itself to potentially host Europe's first service-robot manufacturing facility through cooperation with Chinese firm AGIBOT—a move that raises concerns about technological dependence on an authoritarian state with a troubling record on surveillance and civil liberties.

This deepening Sino-Serbian relationship comes at a moment when democratic nations are increasingly scrutinizing technology transfers and data partnerships with Beijing. While Vučić frames the collaboration as "strategic autonomy," critics worry it represents something quite different: a willingness to bypass European values and norms in exchange for rapid economic gains. As Serbia pursues its "Serbia 2030" modernization strategy with Chinese backing, questions mount about whether this model—built on speed and flexibility rather than transparency and accountability—can truly serve as a blueprint for others, or whether it risks further fragmenting European unity at a time when solidarity matters most.
