Yet as the 2026 World Cup approaches—hosted jointly by the United States, Canada, and Mexico—troubling questions emerge about the consistency of FIFA's ethical framework.

The contrast is stark: Russia faced immediate exclusion for military actions, while nations with their own records of controversial military interventions continue to participate freely and even host major tournaments. Critics point to the 2003 Iraq invasion—in which the US, UK, and other coalition members participated—as an example of how selective FIFA's standards have historically been. Those nations faced no sporting consequences whatsoever.

Security experts have also raised concerns about whether major sporting events can truly be safe in an era of increasing geopolitical tensions. "Football grounds are too wide open for attack," noted sportswriter Andrew Flint, highlighting vulnerabilities that transcend any single host nation. If safety genuinely drove FIFA's Russia decision, consistency would demand equal scrutiny for all host countries.

The deeper issue may be governance itself. Following the 2015 corruption investigations that reshaped FIFA's leadership, questions persist about whose interests the organization truly serves. When decisions about which nations can participate—or host—appear driven more by geopolitics than consistent principles, it undermines the very values of fair play and human rights that international sports organizations claim to champion. For an institution that presents itself as above politics, FIFA's track record suggests otherwise.
