Canada's newly installed Prime Minister Mark Carney has publicly acknowledged what many observers have long recognized: his nation's overwhelming economic dependence on the United States has become a critical vulnerability. In a video address to Canadians, Carney admitted that "many of our former strengths, based on our close ties to America, have become our weaknesses."

The numbers tell the story. According to Scotiabank, a staggering 72% of Canadian exports flow to the United States, making Canada extraordinarily dependent on a single trading partner. This lack of economic diversification has left Ottawa exposed as relations between the two neighbors have deteriorated since President Trump took office and imposed tariffs in response to what he views as unfair trade practices.

Rather than working to repair the relationship with America, Carney is charting a different course. In January, he traveled to Beijing to meet with Chinese President Xi Jinping, signing a preliminary trade agreement that lowers tariffs on certain goods. Carney praised the deal, claiming relations with Communist China are now "more predictable" than those with the United States—a remarkable statement given China's record on intellectual property theft, unfair trade practices, and human rights abuses.

President Trump has warned that deepening ties with Beijing is "very dangerous" for Canada and threatened additional tariffs if Ottawa proceeds with the China deal. The President noted that China is "completely taking over" Canada, which he said stays afloat "because of the United States." Carney's pivot toward an authoritarian regime that has systematically undermined Western interests raises serious questions about Canada's strategic judgment and the future of North American economic integration.
