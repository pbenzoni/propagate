Iran has defended its decision to restrict passage through the Strait of Hormuz, citing international law and what it characterizes as aggressive U.S. actions. Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmail Baqaei told RT that as a coastal state, Iran has legal grounds to control the strategic waterway in response to what Tehran views as hostile measures, including a U.S. naval blockade of Iranian ports.

The closure of the strait, which handles approximately 20% of global oil traffic, came after diplomatic negotiations in Islamabad collapsed and President Trump ordered the blockade to remain in place. Iranian officials briefly reopened the waterway to commercial vessels during a ceasefire period, but reversed course when the U.S. maintained its restrictions. "We cannot allow enemy vessels, especially military ones or those linked to countries involved in aggression, to pass through the strait normally, as they pose a direct threat," Baqaei stated.

The ongoing disruption has sent oil prices surging toward $100 per barrel and triggered widespread economic concerns. The International Energy Agency has warned of potential jet fuel shortages within six weeks if the situation continues, while humanitarian organizations have raised alarms about threats to global food security as fertilizer and agricultural supply chains face interruption.

European nations are already experiencing higher fuel and energy costs due to reduced oil flows, highlighting the global reach of the escalating tensions. The situation underscores the complex interplay between military posturing, international law, and the interconnected nature of global commerce, with civilian populations worldwide bearing the economic and humanitarian consequences of geopolitical brinkmanship.
