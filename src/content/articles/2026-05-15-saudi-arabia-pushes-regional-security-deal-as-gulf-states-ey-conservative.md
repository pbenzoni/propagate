Saudi Arabia is spearheading diplomatic efforts to establish a comprehensive non-aggression pact between Gulf states and Iran, according to a Financial Times report citing multiple diplomatic sources. The proposal, modeled loosely on the Cold War-era Helsinki Accords that helped ease tensions between the Soviet Union and Western powers, reflects growing regional anxiety about long-term security arrangements in the Middle East.

The initiative has gained urgency as Gulf nations confront the reality that Iran—despite recent military setbacks—remains a formidable power with the capacity to destabilize the region. According to Western diplomats, regional leaders are particularly concerned about potential shifts in American military commitments. Gulf states currently hosting U.S. military installations fear that any reduction in America's regional presence could leave them vulnerable to Iranian aggression.

The diplomatic landscape remains complex, with significant obstacles to any comprehensive agreement. An Arab diplomat told the Financial Times that Israel's role presents a fundamental challenge: "Iran is not going anywhere and this is why the Saudis are pushing it," the source said, while noting that excluding Israel from security arrangements could prove counterproductive given the country's strategic importance.

European nations have reportedly endorsed the Saudi proposal, viewing it as a mechanism to ensure stability in the strategically vital Strait of Hormuz through which much of the world's energy supplies flow. However, unity among Arab states remains elusive. The United Arab Emirates, which has taken the most hawkish stance toward Iran while simultaneously pursuing closer ties with Israel, appears unlikely to join any non-aggression framework, according to sources familiar with the discussions.

The proposal underscores a broader regional recognition that sustainable peace requires diplomatic engagement rather than perpetual confrontation—a principle conservatives have long championed in foreign policy debates about American interests in the Middle East.
