The United Kingdom's planned digital identification system is drawing fierce criticism from conservative lawmakers after the government announced it will not include sex or gender markers on the credentials. Prime Minister Keir Starmer's administration says such information is "not necessary" for a system relying on biometric authentication and programmatic verification.

The digital ID scheme, set for full rollout by 2029, was initially planned as mandatory but became optional after nearly 3 million people signed a petition opposing it. The IDs will include name, date of birth, nationality or residency status, and a biometric photo—but not sex or gender designation.

Shadow Equalities Minister Claire Coutinho accused the Labour government of abandoning biological sex as a category, telling GB News: "Having struggled for so long to define what a woman is, Labour have now decided it's easier just to abolish the concept entirely." Former Labour MP Rosie Duffield called the decision "ludicrous," saying it "makes a farce of the whole idea."

The controversy reflects deeper tensions in British politics over gender identity, which has become a flashpoint across sports, healthcare, and public accommodations. Public support for the digital ID plan has plummeted from 57% in July 2025 to just 32% currently, according to an Ipsos poll, with concerns about surveillance and data security alongside the gender debate.

The government maintains that modern biometric technology renders traditional demographic markers like sex unnecessary for verification purposes. Privacy advocates and civil liberties groups have raised separate concerns about the centralized database creating potential vulnerabilities to hacking and government overreach.
