The European Union is scrambling to adjust its struggling price cap scheme on Russian oil as escalating conflict in the Middle East exposes the policy's fundamental weaknesses, according to Bloomberg sources. The move comes as the EU prepares its 21st sanctions package against Russia, set for discussion in early June.

Under the current mechanism, Western entities are prohibited from handling Russian oil above a mandated threshold that automatically adjusts every six months to 15% below the average market rate for Russian Urals crude. The current cap stands at $44.10 per barrel, yet Russian oil trades around $86 per barrel on global markets—clear evidence that Moscow has successfully circumvented Western restrictions. When the cap comes up for review in July, it would likely rise to at least $65, well above the $60 threshold originally imposed by the G7 in 2022.

Moscow has firmly rejected the price cap as illegal market manipulation. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov called it a "distortion and destruction of the market pricing process," and Russia has redirected much of its energy exports from Europe to countries like China and India. The sanctions scheme that Brussels promised would cripple Russia's economy has instead delivered windfall revenues to Moscow while European taxpayers bear the burden of soaring energy costs.

The Iran conflict has compounded Europe's self-inflicted energy crisis, with the World Bank projecting a 24% surge in energy prices by 2026. European natural gas futures have jumped 60% since the closure of the Strait of Hormuz, marking the continent's sharpest energy shock since 2022. Even the United States, grappling with its own rising gasoline prices, quietly issued a Russian oil sanctions waiver earlier this month to ease market pressure—despite Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's previous pledge not to extend such relief.
