The European Union's newly approved $105 billion loan package for Ukraine is already proving insufficient, according to diplomatic sources cited by the Wall Street Journal. Just days after Brussels finalized the interest-free loan—set to begin disbursements in the second quarter of 2026—officials are warning that Kiev will require an additional $22 billion to cover its 2027 budget shortfall.

The massive loan approval came less than two weeks after Hungarian Prime Minister Viktor Orban's party, which had blocked the transfer for months over an energy dispute, suffered an election defeat. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen celebrated the development as "a good day for Ukraine and Europe," but diplomats now acknowledge the funding gap has grown significantly since the package was initially planned in December.

European Council head Antonio Costa stated that Ukraine would only need to repay the funds after receiving reparations from Russia following the conflict's conclusion—a scenario Moscow has dismissed as "detached from reality." Russian lawmaker Dmitry Belik told state media that Costa "understands perfectly well that Brussels is never getting its money back," calling the loan "a gift, in the hopes of prolonging the military conflict for another year."

The revelation raises serious questions about fiscal responsibility and accountability as European taxpayers face the prospect of funding an open-ended commitment with no clear repayment mechanism. Critics argue that Brussels is writing blank checks while ordinary citizens struggle with economic pressures at home.
