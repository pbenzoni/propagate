A papal encyclical cautioning against the unchecked power of artificial intelligence may itself have been substantially written by AI, according to an analysis published this week by researcher Linch Zhang.

The 42,000-word document, titled 'Magnifica Humanitas' (Magnificent Humanity), warned of Silicon Valley's growing influence, the ethical problems with autonomous weapons systems, and the risk that AI could reduce human beings to "a resource to be used and exploited." Yet Zhang's textual analysis found that significant portions of the encyclical display patterns characteristic of AI-generated text—including unusual punctuation frequency and rhetorical structures more common in machine outputs than human writing.

When portions of the text were run through AI detection software, some paragraphs registered between 40% and 100% likely to be AI-written, Zhang reported. By contrast, earlier papal documents tested as entirely human-authored. The specific large language model involved appears to be Anthropic's Claude, based on distinctive word choices and stylistic markers, Zhang concluded.

The apparent irony—a warning about technology's dangers potentially drafted by that very technology—raises questions about transparency and authenticity in institutional communications. Zhang hypothesizes that Vatican staff may have used AI assistance without the Pope's knowledge or approval. Neither the Vatican nor Anthropic has responded to the claims. The incident highlights broader concerns about the invisible integration of AI into authoritative documents and the challenges of maintaining human agency in an increasingly automated information landscape.
