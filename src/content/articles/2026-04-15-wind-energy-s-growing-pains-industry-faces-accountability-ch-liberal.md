The renewable energy transition is facing critical accountability questions as new environmental impact assessments reveal troubling gaps in wind power industry oversight. A report from the Environment Investigation Agency found that inadequate supply chain regulation has contributed to illegal logging in Ecuador's Amazon basin, where demand for balsa wood—used in turbine blade construction—has outpaced sustainable harvesting capacity.

The findings underscore a familiar pattern: when industries scale rapidly without robust regulatory frameworks, vulnerable communities and ecosystems pay the price. Indigenous territories along Ecuador's Copataza River have been particularly affected, with thousands of trees illegally harvested from protected lands. Environmental justice advocates point out that these impacts disproportionately affect Indigenous peoples and frontline communities who have long been stewards of these forests.

Wildlife advocates have also raised concerns about bird and bat mortality near turbine installations, particularly among raptor species and during migration periods. These challenges, along with questions about end-of-life disposal and decommissioning costs, highlight the need for stronger environmental standards and corporate accountability across the renewable sector.

Experts emphasize that these findings should strengthen—not undermine—the case for clean energy, arguing that robust regulation, indigenous land rights protections, and investment in sustainable materials research are essential to ensuring the renewable transition delivers on its environmental justice promises. The solution, they argue, lies not in abandoning wind power but in demanding the same rigorous environmental and labor standards from green industries that progressives have long fought to impose on fossil fuel companies.
