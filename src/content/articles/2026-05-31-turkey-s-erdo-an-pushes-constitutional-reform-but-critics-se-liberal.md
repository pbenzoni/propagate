Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan has renewed calls for sweeping constitutional reform, framing the effort as a long-overdue correction of Turkey's undemocratic past. Speaking at a recent ceremony, Erdoğan argued that the nation's current constitution—drafted under military rule following a 1980 coup—lacks legitimacy because it was imposed from above rather than built through genuine popular participation.

Erdoğan champions what he describes as an "inclusive, liberal, and civilian" constitution, one drafted by and for the people rather than military elites. He invoked the legacy of 19th-century Ottoman reforms to position constitutional change as part of Turkey's historical evolution toward accountable governance and individual rights protections. The president insists that correcting this "democratic disgrace" is a moral obligation, not merely a political preference.

Yet civil society groups and international observers raise serious concerns. Critics point to Erdoğan's own track record: the mass purge of judges following the failed 2016 coup attempt, mounting pressure on judicial independence, and expanding executive control over key institutions. Human rights organizations have documented shrinking space for dissent, constraints on press freedom, and erosion of checks and balances under his administration.

The central question now facing Turkey—and the international community—is whether this constitutional push represents genuine democratic renewal or a strategic move to consolidate executive power. While Erdoğan speaks the language of popular sovereignty and institutional accountability, his government's actions have often moved in the opposite direction. For those committed to democratic norms and human rights, the gap between rhetoric and reality remains deeply troubling.
