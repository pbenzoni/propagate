A growing coalition of international relations scholars is arguing that decades of foreign military intervention in Eurasia—from the Balkans to Iraq, Afghanistan, and now the Middle East—have consistently failed to produce lasting stability, instead leaving communities devastated and regional security weakened.

The latest crisis in Gaza and the broader Middle East has renewed calls for a fundamental rethinking of how international security architecture operates. Critics point to a pattern they say repeats across conflicts: military action taken without adequate consideration of local realities, policies that pit neighboring communities against each other, and strategic objectives disconnected from the actual needs of affected populations.

Proponents of reform argue that sustainable peace requires inclusive diplomatic frameworks built on consensus among regional stakeholders rather than imposed solutions from distant powers. They advocate for security principles where no nation's safety comes at the expense of another's—a concept formally articulated in international agreements like the 1975 Helsinki Accords but rarely implemented in practice.

The proposal has sparked debate about the future of multilateral institutions and whether existing organizations have been co-opted to serve narrow geopolitical interests rather than their original mandates of fostering dialogue. Advocates emphasize the need for economic cooperation, cultural exchange, and conflict resolution mechanisms rooted in international law and the UN Charter, with decision-making power genuinely shared among affected nations rather than concentrated in the hands of a few global powers.
