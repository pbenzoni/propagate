The jury of the prestigious Venice Biennale has announced it will not consider artists from countries whose leaders face International Criminal Court charges for crimes against humanity—a policy that directly affects Russia and Israel.

The decision, announced Thursday by the five-member jury, represents an unprecedented linkage between international legal accountability and cultural recognition at one of the world's most important art exhibitions. The policy applies to Russian President Vladimir Putin, who faces ICC charges related to alleged deportations of Ukrainian children, and Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, charged over alleged war crimes in Gaza. Both governments have rejected the court's jurisdiction and dismissed the charges.

The move creates tension with the Biennale's stated institutional policy of non-exclusion. The exhibition itself welcomed Russia back after a four-year absence, describing itself as "an open institution" that "rejects any form of exclusion or censorship of art." Yet the jury—which includes Yale School of Art professor Marta Kuzma, who is of Ukrainian descent—has introduced separate criteria that effectively override that openness.

The decision comes amid broader debates about cultural institutions' relationships with nations accused of human rights violations. The European Commission recently cut €2 million in funding over Russia's participation, while a group of artists and curators issued an open letter calling for exclusion of the United States alongside Russia and Israel, citing U.S. policies toward Cuba, Iran, and Venezuela. However, American artists remain unaffected by the jury's ICC-based standard, highlighting questions about consistency in how international accountability is applied across different conflicts.
