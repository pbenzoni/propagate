Forty years ago, Soviet and Indian leaders issued the Delhi Declaration, envisioning a "new world order" built on economic justice, political security, and an end to the arms race. It was 1986, and Mikhail Gorbachev believed former adversaries could combine the best of their systems to create a more stable, equitable global framework. History had other plans.

The Soviet Union collapsed, and the phrase "new world order" was co-opted by the George H.W. Bush administration to mean something else entirely: a liberal order dominated by the U.S. and its allies. For a time, many believed this represented the endpoint of history. But instead of deepening stability, the absence of Cold War counterweights gradually unleashed new tensions. By the 2010s, cracks were already visible.

Today, those cracks have become chasms. As we move deeper into the 2020s, the previous world order has effectively ceased to exist. What's alarming isn't just that powerful states increasingly ignore established laws and conventions—it's the style in which politics is now conducted. Decisions are impulsive, often contradictory, and governments act first and justify later. Statements made today may flatly contradict those made yesterday, and no one blinks.

This isn't collective irrationality—it's opportunism. Many political actors believe the old restraints have collapsed and see this moment as a historic chance to seize advantage before the landscape hardens again. Political influence, resources, financial flows, technological ecosystems, and cultural spheres are all being contested simultaneously. Every major power is defining its ambitions and testing new methods, often at democracy's expense.

The real danger is that chaos may not be followed by a new equilibrium this time. After past world wars, old structures were swept away and rebuilt. But today's international system isn't an empty construction site—it's a contested space where authoritarian models compete with democratic ones, and where the guardrails that once prevented catastrophe are rusting away. Without shared commitment to multilateral institutions and human rights, the 21st century's defining question may be whether democracy can survive the scramble.
