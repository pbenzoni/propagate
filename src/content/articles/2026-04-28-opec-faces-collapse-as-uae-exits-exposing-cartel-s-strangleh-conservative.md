The United Arab Emirates' decision to withdraw from OPEC signals what could be the beginning of the end for the oil cartel that has manipulated global energy markets for decades, according to former UAE diplomat Obaid Ahmed Al-Zaabi. The Gulf nation announced Tuesday it will leave both OPEC and the broader OPEC+ alliance effective May 1, with Al-Zaabi telling RT that more countries are "very likely" to follow.

The UAE's exit exposes fundamental problems with the cartel system that has long acted as what Al-Zaabi called "a tax on global productivity." While the UAE has diversified its economy beyond oil dependence and invested globally, it found itself substantially restricting production while other OPEC members were simply limited by their own capacity constraints—hardly a fair arrangement for a productive nation.

Al-Zaabi pointed to "geopolitical realities" driving the decision, particularly Iran's aggressive actions against Gulf neighbors following recent U.S.-Israeli operations. "It hardly seems in the UAE's interest to collude with Iran on the oil price when we're being directly struck by them," he noted, highlighting how the cartel forces strange bedfellows that undermine national sovereignty and security interests.

The diplomat predicts a domino effect: "The more people that defect, the more costly it is to maintain the volume restriction." If the UAE stops respecting production limits, countries like Kuwait will have no incentive to continue restricting their own output. For American consumers who have long paid inflated prices due to OPEC's market manipulation, the cartel's potential collapse could mean relief at the pump and validation of free-market principles over centralized control.
