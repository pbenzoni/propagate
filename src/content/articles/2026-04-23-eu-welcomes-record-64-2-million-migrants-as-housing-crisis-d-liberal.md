The European Union's immigrant population has reached an unprecedented 64.2 million people in 2025, according to a new study from the Berlin-based Center for Research and Analysis on Migration, drawing on Eurostat and UN data. Of this total, approximately 46.7 million were born outside the EU—an increase of more than 2 million from the previous year and a dramatic rise from about 40 million in 2010.

Germany continues to host the largest number of foreign-born residents at nearly 18 million, while Spain has seen the fastest recent growth, welcoming approximately 700,000 new arrivals—roughly one-third of the entire EU's annual increase. The distribution of newcomers remains uneven, with Germany and Spain together accounting for nearly half of all new arrivals, while smaller nations like Malta, Cyprus, and Luxembourg experience the highest inflows relative to their population size.

Yet this growth comes amid a deepening affordability crisis that affects both longtime residents and newcomers alike. Eurostat data reveals that 8.2% of EU residents now spend at least 40% of their disposable income on housing, while 16.9% live in overcrowded conditions and 9.2% cannot adequately heat their homes. Among young people aged 15 to 29, nearly one in ten face severe housing cost burdens. European Council President Antonio Costa acknowledged earlier this year that housing affordability sits "at the core of people's disillusionment with democratic institutions."

The housing squeeze has intensified political tensions around migration policy, even as the EU continues its humanitarian commitment to supporting approximately 4.35 million Ukrainian nationals who have fled their country's ongoing conflict. Critics argue that EU-level spending on migration and border management—roughly 2% of the bloc's seven-year budget—remains insufficient, leaving national governments to shoulder most costs while investment in affordable housing lags far behind need.
