The Department of Energy has selected five private nuclear companies to receive weapons-grade plutonium from America's Cold War arsenal, a move that has alarmed nonproliferation experts and Democratic lawmakers who warn of serious security risks.

Among the firms chosen is Oklo Inc., a California small-reactor developer where Energy Secretary Chris Wright served on the board before joining the Trump administration in February 2025. The selection raises conflict-of-interest concerns as the government prepares to transfer up to 20 metric tons of fissile material—enough for thousands of nuclear weapons—into private-sector hands.

"Trump wants to take enough plutonium for 2,000 atomic bombs and hand it over to private industry just to make his rich buddies happy," said Senator Edward Markey (D-MA). "He might as well sell nuclear weapons at Costco. If this material shows up in Iran, we know who's to blame." Critics argue the program creates dangerous precedents for nuclear security and could incentivize other nations to extract and stockpile plutonium from civilian nuclear waste.

The initiative marks a sharp departure from decades of US-Russian cooperation on safely disposing of Cold War nuclear materials. A similar weapons-uranium program successfully converted enough Russian fissile material for 20,000 warheads into peaceful reactor fuel between the 1990s and 2013. A parallel plutonium agreement was 70% complete when Russia suspended it in 2016 following deteriorating relations after the crisis in Ukraine.

The timing is particularly fraught given ongoing tensions with Iran over its nuclear program. The administration has twice attacked Iran since 2025 over concerns about weapons development, even as it loosens controls on weapons-grade materials at home. Arms control advocates warn the policy undermines America's moral authority on nonproliferation while creating tangible risks that dangerous nuclear materials could fall into the wrong hands.
