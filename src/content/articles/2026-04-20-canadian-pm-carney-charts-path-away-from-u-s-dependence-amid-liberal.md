Canada's heavy reliance on the United States as a trading partner has become a liability rather than an asset, Prime Minister Mark Carney warned this weekend, signaling a major shift in Ottawa's approach to international commerce.

In a video address to Canadians, Carney acknowledged that the nation's historically close economic ties with its southern neighbor—which currently accounts for 72% of Canadian exports—have left the country vulnerable. "Many of our former strengths, based on our close ties to America, have become our weaknesses—weaknesses that we must correct," Carney said. He suggested the days of predictable U.S.-Canada relations may be over, stating bluntly that "the good old days will come back."

The remarks come amid escalating tensions with the Trump administration, which has imposed steep tariffs on Canadian goods while the U.S. president repeatedly refers to Canada as "the 51st state." Carney has firmly rejected such characterizations, insisting his country "will never, ever, in any way, shape, or form, be part of the United States."

Seeking alternatives, Carney traveled to Beijing in January for talks with Chinese President Xi Jinping, signing a preliminary agreement that reduces tariffs on certain goods. The prime minister noted that relations with China have become "more predictable" than those with the U.S., though Trump quickly denounced the move as "very dangerous" and threatened retaliatory 100% tariffs on all Canadian imports.

Carney framed Canada's pivot toward trade diversification as both economically prudent and historically resonant, invoking the War of 1812 when Canadian and British forces repelled an American invasion. "We've faced down threats like this before," he said, emphasizing the need to "strike new partnerships abroad so we can sell into new markets."
