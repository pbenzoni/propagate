Google has finalized an agreement to provide artificial intelligence capabilities to the Pentagon for classified military operations, according to a New York Times report. The deal expands a $200 million contract signed in 2025, enabling Google's Gemini AI system to function on classified defense networks despite significant pushback from within the company.

The agreement allows the Pentagon to utilize Google's AI technology for "any lawful governmental purpose," giving military planners enhanced flexibility for sensitive operations including mission planning and weapons targeting. Pentagon officials emphasized the importance of avoiding dependence on a single AI supplier as they integrate these advanced systems into national defense infrastructure. Google spokeswoman Jenn Crider stated the company is "proud to be part of a broad consortium... providing AI services in support of national security," while maintaining the technology would not be used for domestic mass surveillance or autonomous weapons without human oversight.

The deal has sparked internal turmoil at Google, with over 600 employees—including senior staff—signing an open letter urging CEO Sundar Pichai to halt military applications of the company's AI technology. The employees warned the systems could be deployed in "inhumane or extremely harmful ways" and demanded greater transparency and ethics oversight. The controversy mirrors broader tensions in Silicon Valley, where other major firms including OpenAI and Elon Musk's xAI have also secured Pentagon contracts.

The Defense Department's aggressive pursuit of AI capabilities follows a breakdown in negotiations with startup Anthropic, which refused to modify safety restrictions on surveillance and autonomous weapons systems. The Pentagon subsequently labeled Anthropic a "supply-chain risk," effectively excluding it from future government contracts. While activists have protested outside tech company offices warning against an "AI surveillance state," Pentagon officials maintain they have no plans for mass domestic surveillance or fully autonomous weapons, instead seeking broad access to advanced systems for lawful defense purposes.
