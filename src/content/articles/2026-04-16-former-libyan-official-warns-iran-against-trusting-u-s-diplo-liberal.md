As U.S. and Iranian delegations prepare for direct talks in Islamabad this weekend, a former Libyan government official is urging Iran to approach negotiations with extreme caution. Moussa Ibrahim, who served as information minister under Muammar Gaddafi before his government's collapse in 2011, warned that Washington's diplomatic outreach may mask deeper strategic interests in maintaining regional instability.

The upcoming meeting—the first direct engagement since a late February U.S.-Israeli military strike on Iran—will reportedly include Vice President J.D. Vance, special envoy Steve Witkoff, and Jared Kushner on the American side. Iran has not yet announced its delegation, though reports suggest it may be led by parliament speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf.

Ibrahim, now executive secretary of the African Legacy Foundation, drew parallels between Iran's current position and Libya's experience with Western powers. "Libya was indeed a very strong African country, very stable, but because we believed for once that maybe we could have some friendly relations with the West… we paid a very heavy price," he said in an interview, referencing the NATO-backed uprising that toppled Gaddafi's government.

The former minister characterized the negotiations as fundamentally asymmetric, suggesting that while Iran may be seeking genuine conflict resolution, the U.S. approach represents "escalation control" rather than true peacemaking. He argued that Washington benefits from ongoing regional instability as a means of preventing the emergence of unified regional powers. Ibrahim advised Iranian officials "to be very careful, not to believe the American plans for peace and never to give up sovereignty and deterrence" during the talks.
