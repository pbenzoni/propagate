The BBC faced sharp criticism this week after its flagship public affairs program *Question Time* opened with AI-generated deepfakes of historical figures including Winston Churchill, Mahatma Gandhi, Emmeline Pankhurst, and Frida Kahlo. Host Fiona Bruce introduced the digital recreations before clarifying they weren't real panelists, framing the stunt as "a small insight into the use of technology."

The segment, intended to launch a discussion about artificial intelligence's rapid advancement, backfired on social media where viewers condemned it as trivializing serious concerns about AI-generated misinformation. "Genuinely a joke that I am forced to pay a fee for this dross," wrote one viewer, referencing the mandatory license fees that fund the public broadcaster. Critics argued the gimmick undermined substantive conversation about AI's threats to truth and democracy.

The controversy comes as the BBC grapples with financial pressures and questions about its public value. The broadcaster is losing approximately $1.36 billion annually as audiences migrate to streaming platforms, with over 314,000 households dropping their license fees last year alone. In February, the BBC announced a 10% budget cut amid mounting scrutiny of its editorial choices and declining revenue.

Media critics noted the irony of using deepfake technology—which poses documented risks to electoral integrity and factual discourse—as entertainment rather than examining those dangers critically. The incident has reignited debates about whether publicly-funded media institutions are adapting responsibly to technological change or chasing viral moments at the expense of their civic mission.
