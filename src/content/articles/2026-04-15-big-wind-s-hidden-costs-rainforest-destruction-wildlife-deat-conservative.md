The wind energy industry, long championed by environmental activists and subsidized by billions in taxpayer dollars, faces mounting evidence of serious ecological and economic costs that advocates have downplayed or ignored entirely.

A new report reveals that nearly one million balsa hardwood trees are illegally logged from the Amazon rainforest annually to meet soaring global demand for wind turbine blades. Each turbine requires up to 40 balsa trees for its three massive blades. While balsa once came from sustainable plantations, demand has far outstripped legal supply. The Environment Investigation Agency found that exports surged by 50% following illegal logging in virgin rainforests. In Ecuador, which produces over 90% of the world's balsa, more than 20,000 trees were illegally cut from indigenous territory along the Copataza River in just six months during 2020. Some studies estimate that 75% of trees have been removed from certain areas.

The wildlife toll is equally alarming. Turbine blades spin at speeds approaching 200 miles per hour, killing birds and bats through direct impact or sudden pressure changes. Eagles and hawks face particular danger because they hunt in the same open, wind-swept terrain where turbines are built. Bat deaths peak during migration season when animals travel at exactly rotor height. According to analysis, these installations "kill millions of bats, decimate raptor populations, sweep the air of quadrillions of insects and alter local ecology on both land and sea."

The financial burden extends beyond the environmental damage. Wind turbines last only 20 to 30 years—far shorter than traditional power plants—before requiring expensive decommissioning. While towers contain recyclable metals, the massive fiberglass blades, each the size of a Boeing 747 wing, are difficult and costly to recycle, with many ending up in landfills. The net cost of decommissioning a single turbine ranges from $67,000 to $150,000. Critics worry that developers haven't set aside sufficient funds, leaving property owners and taxpayers to foot the bill. None of these projects would survive in a free market without massive government subsidies to produce expensive, unreliable electricity.
