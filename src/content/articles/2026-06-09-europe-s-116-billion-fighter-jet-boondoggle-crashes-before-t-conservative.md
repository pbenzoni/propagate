A massively expensive European attempt to break free from reliance on American-made fighter jets has officially died on the tarmac, with France and Germany pulling the plug on their joint €100 billion ($116 billion) sixth-generation fighter program after years of bureaucratic gridlock.

The Future Combat Air System (FCAS), launched in 2017 with great fanfare by French President Emmanuel Macron and then-German Chancellor Angela Merkel, was supposed to deliver an advanced combat aircraft after 2040 that could rival the American F-35. Instead, it has become yet another cautionary tale about European governments' inability to execute major defense projects without endless political squabbling and industrial turf wars.

German Defense Minister Boris Pistorius acknowledged the failure bluntly: "It was an ambitious, large European project that has now shattered against reality." The collapse came down to an irreconcilable dispute between the two main contractors—France's Dassault Aviation and Germany-headquartered Airbus Defence and Space—over workshare and who would actually run the program. Despite attempts at mediation as recently as March, neither side could bridge the gap.

The timing is particularly telling. European nations have spent years lecturing about the need to counter threats from Russia and reduce dependence on American military hardware, yet when push came to shove, they couldn't overcome their own bureaucratic dysfunction to actually build anything. The project's failure underscores a fundamental truth: Europe's defense capabilities remain heavily dependent on U.S. technology and industrial know-how, despite all the rhetoric about "strategic autonomy."

For Macron, who personally championed FCAS as a cornerstone of European defense independence, the collapse represents a significant blow to his political legacy. The lesson is clear—ambitious government-led industrial projects, especially those involving multiple countries with competing interests, rarely deliver on their promises. Meanwhile, American defense contractors continue producing the world's most advanced military aircraft that European nations will inevitably keep buying.
