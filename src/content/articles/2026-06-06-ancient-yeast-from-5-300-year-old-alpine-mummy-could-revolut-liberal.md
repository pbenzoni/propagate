Researchers at Italy's Eurac Research have successfully baked sourdough bread using yeast strains recovered from Ötzi the Iceman, a 5,300-year-old mummy discovered in the Italian Alps in 1991. The breakthrough offers promising applications for sustainable food production and climate-resilient agriculture.

Microbiologist Mohamed Sarhan and his team isolated several strains of cold-resistant yeast from the Copper Age mummy's skin, digestive tract, and preserved meltwater. After approximately two weeks of adaptation with regular flour feeding, the ancient microorganisms successfully created a viable sourdough starter. "We made some really good dough with it," Sarhan reported.

The discovery has significant implications for reducing the food industry's carbon footprint. Because these yeasts thrived in Ötzi's glacial preservation environment at around -6°C (21.2°F), they can ferment at refrigerator temperatures—potentially eliminating energy-intensive heating during production and transportation. This cold-adaptation could make food production more sustainable and economically efficient.

The research team is now exploring additional applications, including beer production, and has begun consulting with industry experts. The study revealed that the mummy's microbiome contains multiple layers of microbial life: organisms from Ötzi's lifetime, post-mortem colonizers from the glacier environment, and modern microbes introduced during decades of handling and preservation. Genetic analysis confirmed that the cold-loving yeast strains originated from the glacial ecosystem where the mummy remained frozen for millennia.
