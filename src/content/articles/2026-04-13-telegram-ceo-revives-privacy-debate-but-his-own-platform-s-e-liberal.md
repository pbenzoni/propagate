Telegram founder Pavel Durov has reignited criticism of WhatsApp's privacy practices, calling the Meta-owned messaging app's encryption claims "a giant consumer fraud." In a series of social media posts, Durov argued that WhatsApp's reliance on cloud backups undermines its end-to-end encryption, potentially exposing user data stored on Apple and Google servers to third-party access.

However, security experts point out that Telegram's own encryption model raises serious concerns. Unlike WhatsApp, which encrypts all messages by default, Telegram only offers end-to-end encryption through its optional "Secret Chats" feature. Regular Telegram messages are stored in the company's cloud, where they could theoretically be accessed through legal requests or security breaches—precisely the vulnerability Durov criticizes in competing platforms.

The debate highlights ongoing tensions in tech privacy standards. While WhatsApp does encrypt messages end-to-end by default, its optional cloud backup feature can create vulnerabilities if users don't enable additional protections. Meta maintains that its encryption claims are accurate and has called recent lawsuits challenging these assertions "frivolous." Privacy advocates argue that users deserve transparent information about how their data is stored and who can access it, regardless of which platform they choose.

Durov's pointed criticism of Big Tech's data practices resonates with growing concerns about corporate surveillance and user privacy. Yet his own platform's encryption shortcomings underscore a broader industry challenge: balancing user convenience, data accessibility, and genuine privacy protection in an era of increasing digital surveillance threats.
