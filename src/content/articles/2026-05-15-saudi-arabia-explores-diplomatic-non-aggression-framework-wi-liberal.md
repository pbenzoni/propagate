Saudi Arabia is leading an initiative to establish a non-aggression pact between Gulf states and Iran, according to diplomatic sources cited by the Financial Times. The proposal comes as regional powers seek to prevent further military escalation and establish a framework for long-term stability in the Persian Gulf.

The Saudi proposal draws inspiration from the Helsinki Process, the 1970s diplomatic initiative that helped reduce Cold War tensions between the Soviet Union and Western nations through dialogue and confidence-building measures. Western diplomats indicate that European nations, concerned about regional stability and energy security through the Strait of Hormuz, have endorsed the Saudi initiative and are encouraging other Gulf governments to participate.

However, the path toward such an agreement faces significant obstacles. An Arab diplomat acknowledged that while most regional states—including Iran, which has long called for reduced U.S. military presence in the region—might support a comprehensive security framework, the absence of Israel from such negotiations remains a critical complication. "Iran is not going anywhere and this is why the Saudis are pushing it," the diplomat noted, emphasizing the need for pragmatic engagement with Tehran.

The initiative reflects the complex web of relationships in the Gulf, where economic interdependence coexists with political tensions. Iran maintains pragmatic ties with Qatar over shared natural gas resources and enjoys relatively warm relations with Oman, which has served as a mediator in international negotiations. Saudi Arabia and Iran restored diplomatic relations in 2023 after years of rivalry, though significant differences remain.

Not all Gulf states are aligned on the approach. The United Arab Emirates has reportedly taken a more hawkish stance and signaled interest in closer ties with Israel, raising questions about whether Abu Dhabi would join a regional non-aggression framework. The diplomatic complexity underscores the challenges of building collective security arrangements in a region marked by overlapping alliances and longstanding disputes.
