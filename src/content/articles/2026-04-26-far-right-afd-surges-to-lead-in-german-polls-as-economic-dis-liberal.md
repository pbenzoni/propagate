Germany's far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) party has reached a troubling new high in polling, garnering 28% support in a hypothetical election scenario—enough to surpass the ruling Christian Democratic bloc led by Chancellor Friedrich Merz, according to a recent Bild/INSA survey.

The poll, conducted between April 20-24 among 1,203 respondents, shows Merz's CDU/CSU coalition dropping to 24%, while the Social Democrats (SPD) hold at 14%. The findings underscore deepening public frustration with the government's handling of Germany's economic challenges, including anemic 0.3% growth in 2025 and back-to-back recessions in 2023 and 2024.

Public confidence in the ruling coalition has collapsed, with 79% of Germans expressing dissatisfaction with government performance, according to separate YouGov polling. Even among CDU/CSU voters, only 34% approve of their coalition's record. Chancellor Merz himself has been rated Europe's most unpopular leader, with a 76% disapproval rating by Morning Consult.

The AfD's rise reflects a worrying pattern across Europe, where economic anxiety and declining living standards have fueled support for extremist movements. Political analysts note that mainstream parties' failure to deliver tangible improvements in voters' lives creates openings for far-right forces that traffic in divisive rhetoric and scapegoating. Germany now faces the prospect of forming a governing majority that excludes the AfD while cobbling together a coalition requiring at least 45% of the viable vote—a mathematical challenge that could further destabilize the country's political landscape.
