The United States expended significant portions of its advanced missile defense arsenal during the recent conflict with Iran—while Israel conserved much of its own stockpile, according to a Washington Post report citing U.S. officials. American forces deployed more than 200 THAAD interceptors, representing roughly half the total U.S. inventory, along with over 100 SM-3 and SM-6 missiles. Meanwhile, Israel reportedly used only 100 Arrow interceptors and 90 David's Sling interceptors, some against less sophisticated threats from Yemen and Lebanon.

Kelly Grieco, a senior fellow at the Stimson Center, told the Post that "the United States absorbed most of the missile defense mission while Israel conserved its own magazines," highlighting what the newspaper described as a "lopsided dynamic." While the Pentagon has acknowledged less than $30 billion in direct spending, independent analysts warn the true cost—including munitions replacement and broader economic impacts—could exceed $1 trillion.

The disparity comes as U.S. interceptor stockpiles have dwindled and domestic production struggles to meet demand. Meanwhile, Iran has reportedly retained approximately 70% of its mobile launchers and missile stockpile, according to earlier reports, and regained access to most underground military facilities previously damaged in strikes. Despite President Trump's public threats to resume hostilities, observers note that repeated extensions of a fragile April ceasefire suggest the administration is seeking an exit from the increasingly costly engagement.

Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian has rejected what he characterized as attempts at "coercion," while Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei indicated talks could advance if the U.S. ends seizures of Iranian vessels and releases frozen funds. Israeli officials, meanwhile, are reportedly pressing Washington to continue military operations while Tehran remains weakened.
