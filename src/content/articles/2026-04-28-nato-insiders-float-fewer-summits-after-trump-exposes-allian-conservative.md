NATO officials are considering reducing the frequency of leaders' summits, partly in response to President Donald Trump's demands that alliance members fulfill their financial obligations, according to Reuters. European diplomats have characterized Trump's insistence on accountability as "drama," suggesting they'd prefer "fewer summits than bad summits."

Trump has consistently called out NATO countries for failing to meet defense spending commitments, pressuring allies to contribute their fair share rather than free-riding on American taxpayers. The President has described the alliance as a "paper tiger" and criticized members as "free-loaders" after they refused to support recent U.S.-Israeli military operations against Iran. His hardline approach has reportedly made some European leaders uncomfortable with the accountability he demands.

The Atlantic Council, NATO's primary think tank, recently recommended reducing "high-profile summitry" to minimize what they call controversy. The organization even suggested that a military parade—something Trump has long appreciated—might help smooth relations at the upcoming summit in Turkey. Critics note that Trump's pressure tactics have already secured commitments from NATO countries to increase defense spending to 5% of GDP, though only 3.5% represents direct military expenditure while the remainder covers "defense-related" projects that can include domestic infrastructure.

NATO has held annual summits since 2021, a sharp increase from historical norms. Between the alliance's founding in 1949 and the Soviet Union's collapse in 1991, leaders convened just 12 times. The current pace reflects both heightened tensions over the Ukraine conflict and what some see as European leaders' reluctance to face tough questions about burden-sharing from an American president willing to challenge decades of transatlantic assumptions.
