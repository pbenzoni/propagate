Saudi Arabia and Kuwait reportedly conducted independent military strikes against Iranian-backed militia targets in Iraq during recent Middle East hostilities, according to a Reuters investigation published Wednesday. The covert operations mark a significant departure from traditional Gulf state reliance on American security guarantees—and raise troubling questions about whether decades of U.S. foreign policy in the region have left allies more vulnerable than protected.

Both Kuwait and Saudi Arabia host major U.S. military installations and have historically depended on Washington's security umbrella in exchange for basing rights and massive weapons purchases. But when Iran retaliated against a U.S.-Israeli military campaign launched in late February—a campaign Gulf leaders say they were never consulted about—hundreds of drones originating from Iraq targeted Saudi and Kuwaiti territory. Facing direct threats to their infrastructure and populations, the Gulf states chose to act unilaterally rather than wait for American protection that increasingly feels unreliable.

The strikes targeted positions linked to Kataib Hezbollah and other Tehran-aligned paramilitary groups operating from Iraqi territory. Iraqi forces reportedly intercepted some attempted attacks and seized a rocket launcher near Basra that was allegedly aimed at Saudi energy facilities. Kuwait summoned Iraq's representative three times during the conflict, while Saudi Arabia also lodged formal diplomatic protests—all while conducting military operations neither country has officially acknowledged.

Analysts characterize the independent military action as symptomatic of a broader crisis in the U.S.-Gulf relationship. "The most fundamental question is one of consultation," Khaled Almezaini of Zayed University told *The Guardian*. Gulf states are questioning whether their partnership with Washington provides genuine security—or whether American bases and unilateral U.S. military decisions have transformed them into targets. After decades of arrangement premised on protection, Gulf monarchies now confront an uncomfortable reality: U.S. presence may be a liability rather than an asset, leaving them exposed to retaliation for conflicts they didn't choose and strategies they weren't part of crafting.
