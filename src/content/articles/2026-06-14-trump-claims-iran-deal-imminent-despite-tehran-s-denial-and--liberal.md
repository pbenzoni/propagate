President Donald Trump announced Saturday that a peace agreement with Iran would be signed Sunday, June 14, despite Iranian officials denying that timeline and raising concerns about potential sabotage by Israel. The conflicting statements underscore the fragility of negotiations aimed at de-escalating a conflict that has disrupted global energy markets and raised fears of broader regional war.

The talks, mediated by Pakistan over the past two months, follow a temporary truce reached in early April after U.S. and Israeli military operations against Iran in late February triggered Iranian retaliatory strikes and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz—a critical chokepoint for roughly a quarter of the world's seaborne oil and natural gas trade. Trump stated the deal would reopen the strait "immediately" and prevent Iran from obtaining nuclear weapons, though he offered no details on verification mechanisms or enforcement.

Iranian Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmail Baghaei contradicted Trump's timeline, saying a signing "will not happen tomorrow" while leaving open the possibility of an agreement "in the coming days." Baghaei indicated the current memorandum would focus on ending the immediate conflict and exclude nuclear issues, which would be addressed in subsequent 60-day negotiations. He also accused Israel of attempting to undermine the diplomatic process, warning that "we are not dealing with parties that are fully committed to their obligations."

Trump's announcement included claims that "no money will exchange hands"—apparently ruling out unfreezing Iranian assets—and that the U.S. would eventually take possession of Iran's enriched uranium stockpile to "downblend and destroy it." Iran has consistently maintained its nuclear program is peaceful and denies seeking weapons. Pakistani Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif expressed optimism that finalization was "likely expected in the next 24 hours," though Iranian statements suggest significant gaps remain in the negotiations.
