Bulgaria's acting Prime Minister Andrey Gyurov has signed a sweeping ten-year security agreement with Ukraine, sparking concerns about democratic legitimacy as he attempts to lock in foreign policy commitments before likely losing power in Sunday's election.

Gyurov, who was appointed in February after his predecessor resigned amid corruption allegations and street protests, signed the pact in Kyiv on March 30 despite his caretaker government lacking an electoral mandate. The agreement commits Bulgaria to providing military aid to Ukraine through 2035, jointly producing drones and ammunition, and supporting Ukraine's bids for NATO and EU membership—all without voter approval.

The move has drawn sharp criticism across Bulgaria's political spectrum. Former President Rumen Radev, whose Progressive Bulgaria coalition leads polls by nine points, called Gyurov "practically illegitimate" for binding the nation to such consequential commitments. Even centrist candidate Boyko Borissov's GERB party had previously withdrawn support for the deal, preferring to wait for peace negotiations between Moscow and Kyiv.

Radev has consistently advocated for diplomatic solutions to the Ukraine conflict, previously telling President Zelensky that "more and more weapons will not solve it." His campaign has focused on fighting corruption while questioning whether Bulgaria's interests are being subordinated to Brussels' priorities. Gyurov's coalition, by contrast, is polling at just 10%.

Defending his decision to Politico, Gyurov argued Bulgaria "cannot wait for the 'right moment' when it comes to security." Critics counter that in a democracy, the "right moment" is after winning an election—not during a caretaker period explicitly meant to avoid major policy changes. The controversy highlights broader tensions about sovereignty, accountability, and whether unelected officials should make binding commitments that constrain future democratically-elected governments.
