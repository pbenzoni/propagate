Local councils across England have issued guidance discouraging residents from displaying their own national flag—the St. George's Cross—on public property ahead of the World Cup, citing concerns about safety, community cohesion, and maintaining a "welcoming environment," according to reports from British media.

The controversy escalated when the Independent Monitoring Boards, which oversees prisons and immigration detention facilities, published a report suggesting that even England flag badges worn by detention center staff could be perceived by detainees as "bias or even intimidation." The report specifically referenced recent protests where national flags were displayed prominently.

Reform UK leader Nigel Farage responded sharply to the guidance, asking on social media whether ordinary citizens who wear England badges or fly the national flag outside their homes are now considered threatening to migrants. Shadow Home Secretary Chris Philp similarly criticized what he called "woke councils," urging them to "stop being ashamed of our national identity and culture, and start celebrating it instead."

The debate centers on 'Operation Raise the Colours,' a grassroots campaign that began in Birmingham in August 2025 and spread across England. Proponents describe the initiative as promoting patriotism and national pride by displaying St. George's Cross and Union flags on lampposts, bridges, and public spaces. Critics, however, have characterized the campaign as linked to anti-immigration sentiment.

The United Kingdom has experienced significant anti-immigration protests in recent years, with citizens and activists voicing frustration over what they view as inadequate border control and insufficient action on crimes involving migrants. Demonstrations have occurred outside hotels housing asylum seekers in Scotland and England, while protests erupted in Belfast this week following the charging of a Sudanese asylum seeker in a local stabbing death.
