Defense technology contractor Anduril Industries has surged to a $61 billion valuation in less than a decade, positioning itself as a critical player in America's defense against an increasingly aggressive China. Founded by Palmer Luckey—the entrepreneur behind Oculus VR—the company has secured more than $20 billion in U.S. military contracts for cutting-edge systems including attack drones, autonomous fighter jets, and AI-powered battlefield networks.

Luckey has been outspoken about the urgency of the China threat, warning military officers at West Point that Beijing plans to seize Taiwan and could then target U.S. allies including Japan and the Philippines. "Everything Anduril builds needs to be built with the assumption that sometime in 2027, China is going to move on Taiwan," Luckey told podcast host Joe Rogan. His assessment draws on intelligence community warnings about Xi Jinping's military modernization efforts, though the 2026 Annual Threat Assessment clarifies that Beijing does not currently plan a 2027 invasion.

However, questions remain about whether Anduril's advanced systems can deliver on their promises when lives are on the line. The company's Altius loitering munitions and Ghost reconnaissance drones—both deployed to Ukraine—encountered significant operational challenges. Ukrainian forces discontinued use of the Altius system in 2024 due to persistent malfunctions, despite the drones costing roughly $400,000 per unit. Ghost drones proved vulnerable to Russian electronic warfare and terrain confusion. Recent U.S. military demonstrations have also revealed technical setbacks across multiple Anduril platforms.

As Anduril prepares to go public and pursue even larger government contracts, the company faces a critical test: proving that its innovative technology can perform reliably in real-world combat scenarios against a sophisticated peer adversary like China. With Taiwan's security and broader regional stability potentially at stake, ensuring these systems work as advertised is not just a business question—it's a matter of national security.
