Saudi Arabia and Kuwait conducted covert military strikes against Iranian-backed militia targets in Iraq during recent Middle East hostilities, according to a Reuters report. The operations mark a significant shift as long-standing American allies in the Gulf increasingly question whether Washington can still be trusted to protect their sovereignty.

Both nations—which host major U.S. military installations—came under sustained missile and drone attacks as Iran retaliated against U.S.-Israeli military operations launched in late February. Hundreds of Iranian drones targeting these American allies reportedly originated from Iraq, including strikes launched by Kataib Hezbollah, a Tehran-linked paramilitary group. Saudi fighter jets struck militia positions in Iraq before the U.S.-Iran ceasefire in early April, while Iraqi sources reported that Kuwait launched missiles at least twice at Kataib Hezbollah targets.

The independent military action reflects mounting frustration among Gulf Cooperation Council states—Bahrain, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, and Oman—which have hosted U.S. bases and purchased billions in American weapons for decades in exchange for security guarantees. Analysts say these nations are increasingly questioning whether their partnership with Washington has become a liability rather than an asset, particularly after the U.S. launched military operations without consulting regional partners or presenting a coherent long-term strategy.

"The most fundamental question is one of consultation," said Khaled Almezaini, an associate professor at Zayed University in Abu Dhabi. Gulf states are asking whether they're "actually achieving the kind of partnership and security support that they feel is necessary if the United States is going to engage militarily in the region." The conflict has raised uncomfortable questions about whether American military presence in the Gulf provides genuine protection or simply makes these nations targets for Iranian aggression.
