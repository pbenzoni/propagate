In a striking display of diplomatic prowess, Chinese President Xi Jinping has welcomed both President Donald Trump and Russian President Vladimir Putin to Beijing in back-to-back state visits this month. The sequence underscores China's rising influence as a global power broker—one that refuses to be boxed into choosing between major powers or playing the kind of zero-sum geopolitics that defined the Cold War era.

Trump's May visit marked his first state trip to China since 2017, while Putin—a more frequent visitor to Beijing—arrived shortly after on May 19 for a two-day summit. The parade of world leaders hasn't stopped there: South Korean President Lee Jae Myung, British Prime Minister Keir Starmer, Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney, and Spain's Pedro Sanchez have all made the journey to China this year, joined by numerous foreign and defense ministers. The sheer volume and diversity of these visits signals Beijing's expanding global reach and its stated principle of "non-alignment, non-confrontation and non-targeting of third parties."

For American conservatives focused on protecting national sovereignty and economic vitality, China's balancing act poses both opportunity and challenge. China-US trade totaled $414.69 billion in 2025 despite tensions, while China-Russia trade reached $228.1 billion and is growing at nearly 20% year-over-year. The economic stakes are enormous, especially as the U.S. continues to lead in critical technologies like semiconductor design and quantum computing. The question facing American policymakers: Can Washington engage Beijing on trade and security without empowering a partnership between China and Russia that could undermine American interests?

Some analysts argue that the old Henry Kissinger playbook—leveraging ties with China to counterbalance Russia—may be outdated in an era where Beijing and Moscow have drawn closer together as strategic partners. Yet Trump's willingness to meet with Xi suggests a pragmatic recognition that isolating China entirely is neither feasible nor necessarily in America's interest. The challenge for U.S. leadership will be ensuring that engagement with Beijing protects American workers, defends intellectual property, and upholds the rules-based international order that has underpinned decades of prosperity and security.
