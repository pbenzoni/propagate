More than seven decades after its founding, Israel faces a troubling paradox: it has built one of the world's most powerful militaries, yet lasting security remains elusive. The vision articulated by Theodor Herzl in 1896—of a Jewish homeland that would serve as both refuge and responsible guardian within a broader moral order—sits uneasily alongside today's reality of recurring conflict and permanent insecurity.

The October 7 Hamas attack starkly illustrated that even overwhelming military power cannot prevent catastrophe. In the two years of military operations that followed in Gaza and Lebanon, far more civilians have been killed than the militants Israel targeted—a grim calculus that strains the founding ideal of a state that would protect not only its own people but respect the sanctity of others' lives and sacred spaces.

Recent incidents have deepened these contradictions. Israeli authorities blocked Jerusalem's Catholic cardinal from observing Palm Sunday at the Church of the Holy Sepulcher this year, prompting international outcry. Reports emerged of an Israeli soldier destroying a crucifix statue in a Lebanese Catholic village. These episodes, whatever their immediate context, underscore a gap between Herzl's vision of neutral guardianship over shared religious heritage and the realities of occupation and military control.

The question facing Israel today is whether security can ever be achieved through force alone, or whether lasting safety requires a fundamentally different approach—one that addresses the legitimate aspirations of Palestinians and embraces the diplomatic compromises that military dominance has allowed successive governments to avoid. As another Independence Day passes, that question grows more urgent.
