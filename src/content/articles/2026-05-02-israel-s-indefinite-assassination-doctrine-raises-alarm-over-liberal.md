International legal scholars are sounding the alarm over what they describe as an unprecedented erosion of established norms governing armed conflict. Israel's stated intention to assassinate not only current Iranian leadership but future successors—individuals not yet identified or in office—marks what experts characterize as a dangerous departure from principles of individual accountability and due process.

This approach, which some analysts term "role-based targeting," removes the traditional link between punishment and individual conduct. Rather than responding to specific actions by identifiable persons, the policy threatens anyone who might assume a particular governmental position, regardless of their future decisions or policies. Legal experts argue this violates fundamental human rights principles that require accountability to be tied to individual culpability, not institutional roles.

The implications extend beyond the immediate Iran-Israel conflict. Critics warn that normalizing such open-ended assassination doctrines could fundamentally undermine international humanitarian law, potentially licensing states to bypass judicial processes entirely. Civil liberties advocates emphasize that extrajudicial killing—particularly when divorced from specific individual actions—represents a form of collective punishment incompatible with democratic values and the rule of law.

While supporters frame the approach as deterrence strategy, human rights organizations counter that weaponizing political office itself sets a destabilizing precedent. The policy raises profound questions about sovereignty, accountability, and whether military force can legitimately target abstract roles rather than concrete individuals who have committed identifiable acts.
