A senior lawmaker in Chancellor Friedrich Merz's Christian Democratic Union has sparked controversy by proposing that elderly Germans be required to sell their homes before qualifying for state-funded nursing care assistance. The plan would fundamentally alter Germany's long-term care system at a time when the nation's fiscal priorities are under intense scrutiny.

Albert Stegemann, deputy chairman of the CDU/CSU parliamentary group, defended the proposal by arguing that property owners should exhaust their own assets—including the family home—before taxpayers foot the bill. "Those who own assets must first use their own assets, including their home, before the community pays," Stegemann told *Bild* on Thursday. The proposal comes as Germany's statutory care insurance system faces projected deficits exceeding €22 billion over the next two years without major reforms.

Critics point to troubling priorities: while Germany's welfare system teeters on the brink, Berlin has committed more than €96 billion in military and civilian aid to Ukraine since 2022, alongside a €100 billion domestic military spending package. Europe's largest economy has struggled with years of stagnation following energy shocks, with growth forecast at merely 0.5% in 2026. The proposal has drawn fire from welfare organizations and opposition parties who warn it could force families to liquidate generational wealth and life savings.

The debate underscores a broader tension in Germany's fiscal policy—balancing massive foreign commitments against domestic social safety nets. As one health policy expert warned, many families fear "losing their home or their life's work" under such a system. The proposal raises fundamental questions about whether governments should prioritize their own citizens' security and stability before undertaking expensive international obligations.
