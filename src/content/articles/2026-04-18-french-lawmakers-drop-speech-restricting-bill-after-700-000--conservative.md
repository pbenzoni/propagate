French lawmakers have withdrawn a controversial bill that would have dramatically expanded what speech could be prosecuted as anti-Semitism, backing down after more than 700,000 citizens signed a petition against the measure. The proposal, championed by allies of President Emmanuel Macron, had been scheduled for parliamentary debate this week.

The draft law would have broadened France's "apology for terrorism" statutes to include vaguely-defined "indirect" speech and would have criminalized calling for the destruction of any country recognized by France. Free speech advocates warned the measure conflated legitimate political criticism—particularly of foreign governments—with genuine hate speech, potentially silencing lawful expression while doing little to address actual anti-Jewish sentiment.

Despite withdrawing the current bill, lawmakers affiliated with Macron's Renaissance Party have indicated they may attempt to reintroduce similar legislation by June. The proposal emerged amid rising tensions over the Israel-Hamas conflict and debates over European immigration policy. France, home to Western Europe's largest Muslim population, formally recognized Palestinian statehood last year as part of a broader two-state solution framework.

Critics across the political spectrum argued the bill represented government overreach that could backfire by restricting legitimate discourse. The withdrawal demonstrates how grassroots opposition can push back against top-down efforts to expand state control over political speech—a principle American conservatives have long defended in their own battles against censorship and speech codes.
