Instead, Canadian officials allowed it to land in Montreal, let everyone disembark, and simply put the risky passenger on the next flight back to Paris.

The incident raises serious questions about sovereignty and national security. Why should Canada absorb health risks that American border authorities rightly rejected? The passenger hadn't cleared customs anywhere, meaning the plane could have been turned away without anyone technically entering U.S. territory. Yet rather than enforce their own borders, Canadian officials rolled over—prioritizing diplomatic niceties over the safety of their own citizens.

This isn't an isolated case of weakness. Canada previously arrested Huawei executive Meng Wanzhou at America's request, triggering a years-long diplomatic crisis with China that served Washington's interests while leaving Ottawa holding the bag. The pattern is clear: Canada's leadership repeatedly subordinates national interests to global pressure.

The contrast with America's approach under strong leadership is striking. While the U.S. protects its borders and puts citizens first—whether through tariffs, immigration enforcement, or rejecting health risks—Canada's government seems more concerned with maintaining its reputation as the "nice guy" on the world stage. That approach may win applause from international bureaucrats, but it leaves Canadian citizens vulnerable and their sovereignty compromised. True allies respect each other's boundaries; they don't expect one nation to absorb problems the other has the sense to reject.
