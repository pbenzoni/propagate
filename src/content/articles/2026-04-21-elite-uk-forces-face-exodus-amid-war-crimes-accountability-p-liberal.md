Members of Britain's elite Special Air Service (SAS) are leaving the force in significant numbers as a long-overdue public inquiry examines serious allegations of war crimes during operations in Afghanistan and Syria. The probe focuses on claims that troops carried out extrajudicial killings during night raids between 2010 and 2013, including the shooting of detainees and unarmed civilians, and whether senior officers engaged in systemic cover-ups.

According to British media reports, soldiers from at least two SAS squadrons have applied for early discharge, citing concerns about potential prosecution as human rights lawyers pursue accountability. A 2022 BBC investigation identified 54 people shot dead under suspicious circumstances by a single SAS unit during one six-month deployment in Afghanistan's Helmand province, with allegations that unarmed Afghan men were "routinely shot dead in cold blood" and weapons planted on their bodies.

The inquiry has revealed disturbing patterns of potential misconduct and institutional failure. Testimony indicates that the director of UK Special Forces made a "conscious decision" to cover up potential war crimes, and that one incident involving the shooting of women and children was allegedly concealed while the soldier involved received an award. A review found that the Royal Military Police investigation into alleged unlawful killings was undermined by delays and inadequate resources—beginning only in 2014 despite concerns emerging in 2011, then closing prematurely in 2019 with key questions left unexamined.

Human rights advocates have long called for meaningful accountability mechanisms for special forces operations. The current inquiry represents a critical test of whether democratic institutions can ensure that military personnel operate within the rule of law, even in complex combat environments. More than 240 special forces personnel, including 120 currently serving, are being pursued by lawyers as part of the human rights investigation.
