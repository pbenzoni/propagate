A timely new book arrives as America grapples with deepening political crises and the fraying of international institutions that once anchored democratic values worldwide. *After Nations: The Making and Unmaking of a World Order* by Rana Dasgupta offers a sweeping historical analysis of how Western nation-states—particularly Britain and the United States—rose to global dominance through capitalist expansion and colonial extraction, only to find themselves hollowed out by the very systems that empowered them.

Dasgupta's diagnosis is sharp: liberal democracies are failing their citizens, unable to address systemic inequality, climate crisis, or the erosion of social trust. The American-led world order established after World War II is collapsing under the weight of its own contradictions. Where the analysis falters, however, is in its proposed remedy. The book's final chapter envisions a post-national future enabled by digital technology—a "universal digital citizenship," planetary governance, and an eco-friendly reimagining of political community.

Yet this vision feels disconnected from the book's own insights. Dasgupta acknowledges that authoritarian states like China and Russia demonstrate resilience precisely because they've rejected liberal democratic norms. If the crisis he describes is specific to liberal democracies, why assume the nation-state itself will disappear? And perhaps most troublingly, his faith in tech giants as agents of progressive transformation ignores how these same corporations have deepened inequality, undermined privacy, and concentrated unprecedented power in unaccountable hands.

The core value of *After Nations* lies not in its utopian conclusion, but in its unflinching account of how democracies have betrayed their founding promises. For readers concerned about preserving democratic institutions and building a more just society, Dasgupta's historical analysis offers crucial context—even if his prescriptions require considerable skepticism.
