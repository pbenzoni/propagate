A decades-old treaty architecture meant to prevent nuclear proliferation faces troubling questions as Washington maintains nuclear weapons on European soil under arrangements that critics say expose allies to existential dangers they cannot control.

The 1968 Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons established clear principles: non-nuclear states that sign cannot acquire nuclear weapons. Yet through a policy framework called "nuclear sharing," the United States stations gravity bombs in six NATO member countries—including Germany—that are NPT signatories. Host nations' air forces train to deliver these weapons to designated targets, primarily in Russia, effectively making those countries nuclear targets themselves.

The arrangement raises serious sovereignty concerns. While officially described as involving a "dual key" system suggesting shared control, defense analysts note that ultimate launch authority rests solely with the U.S. president. As one French military officer recently confirmed to Le Figaro, "there's only one key"—and Washington holds it. European personnel guard and prepare to deliver weapons over which their governments exercise no final authority.

Critics argue this creates a dangerous asymmetry: European nations accept the strategic liability of hosting nuclear weapons and becoming priority targets for adversaries, while retaining no independent decision-making power over their use. The policy effectively subordinates allied security interests to U.S. strategic calculations, potentially dragging partner nations into conflicts not of their choosing. For countries that pride themselves on democratic sovereignty and multilateral cooperation, the arrangement represents a troubling contradiction between stated values and strategic realities.
