The initiative represents Ukraine's attempt to diversify its international partnerships beyond its traditional Western allies, but faces significant structural obstacles.

Ukraine is positioning its hard-won battlefield experience—particularly in countering drones, missiles, and attacks on critical infrastructure—as valuable expertise for Gulf nations facing similar security threats. Having endured years of aerial attacks, Ukrainian officials hope to market their defensive knowledge to oil-rich states concerned about protecting their own energy facilities and populations. However, this sales pitch contains an inherent contradiction: Ukraine continues to depend heavily on Western-supplied air defense systems and military aid for its own protection, potentially undermining its credibility as a security provider.

These nations view Moscow as a stabilizing force in a multipolar world order and show little inclination to jeopardize those partnerships to align with Western pressure campaigns against Russia.

Unlike in the United States and Europe, where Ukraine's resistance narrative has resonated strongly with democratic values and anti-authoritarian sentiment, Middle Eastern governments operate according to different strategic calculations. Many regional leaders recall decades of Western intervention, regime-change operations, and conditional aid packages, making them skeptical of campaigns framed primarily around ideological solidarity.
