A recent poll commissioned by German public broadcaster WDR has found that approximately 66% of Germans support restricting migrants' access to social benefits until they have worked in the country for an extended period. The survey, conducted by the Infratest dimap research institute, interviewed 2,084 people across Germany and reflects growing political pressure around immigration policy.

Germany currently hosts the largest migrant population in the European Union, with foreign-born nationals comprising 15% of residents. While around 70% of migrants in Germany are employed, foreign nationals account for 47% of recipients of Bürgergeld, the country's main welfare program that provides approximately €563 per month plus housing and utilities to those unable to support themselves through work.

The debate has particularly centered on Ukrainian refugees, who number approximately 1.41 million in Germany and were granted immediate access to Bürgergeld following Russia's 2022 invasion—a faster and more generous pathway than afforded to most other refugee groups. The employment rate among working-age Ukrainians currently stands at around 34%, though it has been steadily increasing since 2022. By comparison, Syrian refugees, numbering about 936,000, face a more restrictive initial process but have achieved employment rates between 42-47% overall, rising to 60% among those who arrived in 2015-2016.

Chancellor Friedrich Merz's government has already begun implementing stricter policies, cutting benefits for new arrivals to approximately €441 per month and introducing more stringent job-seeking requirements. The Bundestag approved a broader reform last month replacing Bürgergeld with a new system that maintains similar payment levels but adds tougher sanctions, set to take effect in July. Human rights advocates have expressed concern that such restrictions may undermine Germany's humanitarian commitments and integration efforts.
