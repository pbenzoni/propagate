The latest measures designate 56 entities connected to Russia's military-industrial complex, including 17 located in China, the United Arab Emirates, Belarus, and Central Asia, alongside tightened export controls on 60 additional entities allegedly supplying Russia's defense sector.

The expanded scope represents Europe's determination to close loopholes that allow dual-use goods—civilian products with potential military applications—to reach Russian forces. However, China quickly condemned the move as "long-arm jurisdiction," arguing that the EU is overstepping its authority by sanctioning Chinese companies operating outside European borders.

Beijing responded within 24 hours by placing seven European defense firms on its own control list, citing their arms sales to Taiwan. China views this as a coordinated network linking Taiwan, Europe, and Ukraine in what it perceives as a containment strategy.

The escalating tit-for-tat highlights the growing complexity of economic statecraft in an interconnected world.
