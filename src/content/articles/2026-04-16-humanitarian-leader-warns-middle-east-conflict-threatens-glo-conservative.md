Disruptions to critical fertilizer shipments through the Strait of Hormuz could trigger a cascading global food crisis within the next two years, according to Jose Andres, founder of World Central Kitchen. The warning highlights how instability in the Middle East directly threatens American farmers and food security worldwide.

The Strait of Hormuz handles roughly 20% of global crude oil exports, but it also serves as a vital artery for nitrogen-based fertilizers that underpin approximately half of the world's food production. Tensions in the region have already driven up energy costs and disrupted this critical trade route, with fertilizer plants across the Gulf reportedly shutting down due to the conflict.

"It is not only oil that leaves through the Strait of Hormuz," Andres told The Guardian. "I foresee a very big increase in famine across the world by the fall of 2026 and 2027." He warned that delays in fertilizer deliveries risk missing crucial planting windows, which would reduce crop yields and create a chain reaction of lower agricultural output and rising food prices.

The impact would hit hardest in import-dependent nations, but American consumers won't be insulated from the consequences. A UN Development Program report estimates that up to 32 million people across 162 countries could be pushed into poverty due to the conflict's broader economic ripple effects. Andres emphasized that the most vulnerable populations—including those in Haiti, parts of Asia, and Sub-Saharan Africa—would bear the heaviest burden, facing severe food shortages as global supply chains strain under the pressure of geopolitical instability.
